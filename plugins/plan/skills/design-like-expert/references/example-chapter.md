# Example: a chapter on the idea behind a design

Made inside a Fuji grilling where Ash planned how to keep Udyaan students working without pay. Ash approved it. Match its shape, its plainness, its steps that say what happens inside the person, and its one short line for a study. The numbers are made up.

---

## How do you get people to keep working when nobody pays them or makes them?

**Where you are now**
- **The plan gives students a problem and a deadline, and nothing they asked for.** About 80 of every 100 joined hoping for an internship, so the work could feel like homework by Day 8 (say).
- **Money is off the table.** Trophies, certificates and the work itself are all you have to motivate with. Used carelessly, they could stop meaning anything after one batch.
- **You run on your own fuel.** You learn for the joy of it, so the plan quietly assumes students do too.

**After you read this, you can design why someone keeps working: students now, your own team later.**

**What usually happens**
| When | What happens | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** | Students pick a problem off a list | It feels like a task someone handed them |
| **Day 5** | A trophy is announced for the best project | Three teams who think they can win push harder; the rest decide it isn't for them |
| **Day 20** | The trophies were handed out on Day 14 | **Cost:** posts drop to almost nothing, because the thing they worked for is over |

**Where the problem lies**

**Udyaan hands over the reason to work along with the task, and that reason ends when the prize does.**

**The idea that fixes it**

**Find out what each person already wants, and make the work the way they get it. Show them who the work helps and how far they've moved. Give prizes afterwards, to say "you were good."**

Picture learning to cook. If your mum makes you help in the kitchen, you stop the day she stops asking. If you move into a hostel, get sick of canteen food and learn because you want to eat well, you keep cooking. It's the same cooking, but the reason now lives inside you. Psychologists call this move of the reason, from outside a person to inside them, **internalization**.

**How it works, from the first form to month three**

1. **Ask what they want, and let them say it themselves.** People believe a reason they said out loud more than one they were told. A student who types "I want an internship by summer" has made a small promise to themselves.
2. **Tie the work to that want.** In the student's head, "Udyaan's task" becomes "my way to an internship." The project turns into something they'd do even if nobody checked.
3. **Show them the person the problem hurts.** If a problem has a face, quitting feels like letting someone down. If it's only a sentence, it feels like homework.
4. **Let them watch themselves move.** People keep going towards a goal that looks close and reachable, and they give up on one that looks far and vague.
   - **Small wins fuel the day:** a fix closed or a quote collected gives a reason to open the dashboard tomorrow.
   - **A bar that starts partly filled pulls harder than an empty one:** keeping going after "I've started" is easier than starting from "I'll start."
5. **Give rewards afterwards, as news.** When a reward comes changes what it does inside the person:

```
PROMISED BEFORE: "Do this and you'll get a trophy"
  in their head → "I'm doing this for the trophy"
  trophy won, lost or out of reach → effort stops

GIVEN AFTER: "You cleared the bar. Here's what you did well."
  in their head → "I'm good at this"
  effort keeps going, and the trophy means something
```

Founded on: children who loved drawing drew less once a certificate was promised for it. Why it matters: a trophy promised in advance can turn the work into a job, and a small payment turns "I'm helping" into "I'm being underpaid."

| | Today | With internalization |
|---|---|---|
| Why they start | We hand them a problem | They tell us what they want, and the problem is how they get it |
| Why they keep going | The mentor checks on them | They see who it helps and how far they've moved |
| What the trophy is | The goal | The news that they were good |

**How you'd build it, and what changes for you**
- **A first conversation that asks what they want.**
- **A problem card with a real person on it,** plus what solving it gets the student.
- **A dashboard that shows progress:** fixes closed, quotes collected, a bar that starts partly filled.
- **Awards with a bar, given afterwards.** You announce what good work looks like, never the prize as the reason to do it.

What changes for you:
- **Ask before you tell,** with students now and with hires later.
- **Make trophies rare,** so they still mean something.

**How it looks when you use it**
> A student on Day 9 asks: "Why should I keep going, nobody's paying me?"
> The check-in shows their own answer from the first form: "I want an internship by summer."
> Next to it: "3 of 4 fixes done, 5 farmer quotes. That's your interview story."
> Cost: one line added to a check-in that already runs.

**Where else the same idea works**

**Far away: Alcoholics Anonymous.**
- **Staying sober is the member's own want.** Nobody can want it for them.
- **AA hands out a chip at 30 days, 90 days and a year,** after the member got there, in front of the group. The chip is news, and it means something because it was earned and seen.
- **A sponsor and the weekly meeting** are the people who keep a member coming back.

**In your world: your future company.**
- **When you hire someone, ask what they want from the next two years,** and make the role their road to it.
- **Fuji's progress bar is step 4,** which you run on yourself.

**When it isn't enough**
- **Dull, easy-to-count work needs pay.** For work like data entry, inner reasons don't stretch far enough, and paying for each piece done works. Economists call that piece rates.
- **One mentor can't hold 50 students' reasons.** Past about 15 students per mentor, last batch's students mentor the next one. That's called near-peer mentoring.
- **The test:** if the task is dull and countable, pay for it. If it needs thinking and care, build the reason inside the person.

**The answer**

**You keep people working by finding what they already want and making the work their way to it, then showing them who it helps and how far they've moved.** Rewards still work when they come afterwards, as news that someone was good. The same holds for a student with a farm problem, a hire in your company, and you with Fuji: that's internalization.
