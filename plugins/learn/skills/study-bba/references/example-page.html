How do you find out whether each sale makes your startup money or quietly loses it?

Startup money, the first problem · about 50 minutes with the worked examples

Where you are now

  • You judge a month by whether the bank balance went up. So what: you can't tell a slow month from a product that loses money.
  • When someone says "we make 25%", you can't say 25% of what. So what: you could end up with a 20% margin when you meant 25%.
  • Kiran's Oct 9 paper asks you to tell four pairs of money words apart, each with a number. So what: you lose marks if you mix up the definitions.

After this chapter, you can

Look at any product, order or discount and say whether it adds money to the business, and how much. You'll know which costs to count against a sale, how to read a profit percentage, and when a cheap extra order is safe.

What this looks like for you

A scenario, made up for practice. Thread Lab is two students who sell custom hoodies to college clubs at $50 each.

Each hoodie costs $20: $12 for the plain unprinted hoodie, $5 for printing and $3 for delivery.

Every month the studio rent and two small salaries cost $12,000, whatever they sell.

The symptom: March sold 500 hoodies and made money. April sold 300 and the account fell by $3,000. One founder says the hoodies are underpriced.

What usually happens

  1. April closes $3,000 down, and the founders share the $12,000 out across its 300 hoodies. That puts $40 of rent on each, so a hoodie "costs" $60 against a $50 price.
  2. They decide each hoodie loses $10 and stop taking small club orders. May sells 200 hoodies.
  3. May closes $6,000 down. Each hoodie still added $30, but 200 of them filled only $6,000 of the $12,000.

Where the problem lies

The founders judged each hoodie with the rent mixed in. The rent stays the same whether one hoodie sells or none, so it tells you nothing about whether a hoodie earns.

Why businesses split their costs before judging a sale

Businesses with big fixed bills kept dropping products in slow months. Shared-out rent made every product look like a loser. Each product dropped left the same rent on fewer products, so the next one looked worse too.

The fix was to sort costs by one test before judging anything. Costs that rise with each unit sold go on one side. Costs that stay the same however much you sell go on the other.

Charging each unit only its own costs became a method of accounts. Only the costs that move with sales are charged to each unit; the fixed bills are charged to the month as one lump. This is called marginal costing, and it is the method Kiran teaches in Unit 2.

The idea that fixes it

For each sale, take away only the costs that sale itself causes from its price. What is left is what that sale adds toward rent and salaries. If it is above zero the sale helps, and the month turns a profit once those amounts add up past the rent and salaries.

Think of a jar with a line painted on it at $12,000. Every hoodie drops $30 into the jar. Nothing in the jar is profit until it fills past the line, and everything above the line is.

What this means in practice:

  • Judge a sale by price minus its own costs, never by a shared-out rent. April's hoodies each added $30, so they were never the problem.
  • Judge the month by whether the jar passed the line. April filled the jar to $9,000, short of $12,000, because there were too few sales.

Drawn to scale: each bar is what the month's hoodies dropped into the jar.

Four questions finance people ask about every sale

Each question below is the reason one of Unit 2's ideas exists. The question comes first, then what happens with Thread Lab's numbers, then the name Kiran uses. Each ends with a worked example and a twin for you to solve.

Question one

Which of your costs would vanish if you sold nothing this month?

The plain hoodie, the printing and the delivery would vanish. They happen only when a hoodie sells, so they rise with every sale. These are called variable costs.

The rent and salaries would stay. Costs you pay even at zero sales are called fixed costs.

Price minus the variable costs of one unit is what one sale adds. For Thread Lab, $50 minus $20 leaves $30 per hoodie. That $30 is called the contribution per unit, and Kiran's formula is contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit.

Add up the month's contributions, then take away the fixed costs, and what remains is profit. Write it in this order every time; "Less:" means subtract, and a number in brackets is money going out. This layout is the marginal costing statement.

LineMarch, 500 hoodiesApril, 300 hoodies

Contribution$15,000$9,000
Less: fixed costs($12,000)($12,000)

The contribution line has a second name: the contribution margin. In March it is $15,000, and profit is $3,000. Kiran's key uses exactly this: contribution margin = sales − variable costs, and profit = contribution margin − fixed costs.

Either line can be read as a share of sales. $15,000 ÷ $25,000 = 60% is the contribution margin ratio. $3,000 ÷ $25,000 = 12% is the profit margin.

Question two

Which orders add less than nothing?

A club wants 40 hoodies by Friday at the usual $50. Rush printing costs $18 a hoodie and a same-day courier $15, on top of the usual $20: $53 a hoodie in all. Each rush hoodie brings in $50.

Each rush hoodie takes $3 out of the jar. The order brings money in, so nobody notices, and every extra rush hoodie makes the month $3 worse. A sale whose own costs beat its price has a negative contribution, and that is how a sale quietly loses money.

The fix is a lowest price you accept for rush work. At $54 or more, each rush hoodie adds at least $1 toward the line. That lowest price is called a price floor.

Question three

When someone says "25% profit", is it 25% of the cost or 25% of the price?

Thread Lab adds a varsity jacket that costs $80 in total, every cost counted, and sells for $100. The profit is $20 a jacket. Measured against the $80 cost, $20 is 25%, and this is called the markup.

Measured against the $100 price, the same $20 is 20%, and this is called the margin. When a sale makes a profit, the price is bigger than the cost, so the margin is the smaller number of the two.

The common mistake is adding 25% to cost when you wanted a 25% margin. $80 plus 25% is $100, which gives only a 20% margin. For a true 25% margin, price = cost ÷ (1 − margin) = $80 ÷ 0.75 = $106.67.

Question four

What does one more unit cost you, compared with the average unit?

A club orders 100 embroidered hoodies. Setting the machine up for their logo costs $1,000 once, however many hoodies the order holds, so within this order it is a fixed cost. Each hoodie then costs $40.

The 100 hoodies cost $5,000 in total, so each one costs $50 on average. Total cost divided by units is called the average cost.

Making a 101st hoodie adds only its own $40, because the setup is already paid. Total cost goes from $5,000 to $5,040. What one more unit adds to total cost is called the marginal cost.

The $40 marginal cost is exactly one hoodie's variable cost. Marginal costing is named after it: the method charges each unit only what one more unit causes.

So one extra hoodie at $45 is a good deal. Against the average it looks like a $5 loss, but the business gains $45 and spends $40, so it ends $5 better off.

Selling all 100 at $45 is a bad deal. You bring in $4,500 against $5,000 of cost. Price one extra unit against the marginal cost; price the whole order against the average.

Worked example: average and marginal cost

Making 200 tote bags costs $3,000, and making 201 costs $3,012. A shop wants 20 extra bags at $13 each. Should Thread Lab accept, and could it sell all 200 at $13?

StepWorking
Average costtotal cost ÷ units = $3,000 ÷ 200 = $15
Marginal cost$3,012 − $3,000 = $12
Extra order$13 − $12 = $1 a bag, × 20 = $20 gained. Accept.
All 200 at $13200 × $13 = $2,600 against $3,000 of cost, a $400 loss. Refuse.

Accept an extra order when its price beats what one more unit costs. Sell the whole batch (every unit made in one run) only above the average cost, or it loses money.

How it works, from one hoodie order to the month's profit

  1. An order arrives at $50 a hoodie, and you take away only what that hoodie causes: $20. What is left, $30, is its contribution.
  2. Every order's contribution goes into the month's total. 500 hoodies make $15,000.
  3. You take the fixed $12,000 off the total once. What remains, $3,000, is the month's profit.

How you'd build it

One sheet with three parts is enough. A Google Sheet works; nothing fancier is needed until you have many products.

  • A cost list with one extra column: "does this rise with each sale?" Yes means variable, no means fixed.
  • One line per product or order type: price minus variable cost. Any line below zero is an order that quietly loses money.
  • A monthly statement in Kiran's order: sales, variable costs, contribution, fixed costs, profit. Claude can build it from the cost list.

How you'd run it

  1. Month one: you sort every cost and find the hoodie adds $30.
  2. Month two: a rush order shows minus $3 a hoodie, and you quote $54 instead.
  3. Month three: you filed the $3 delivery as fixed, so contribution read $33. You move it back to variable and the number drops to $30.

When to skip it

If you have one product, one price and no fixed bills worth naming, skip the split. Money in minus money out tells you the same thing.

The answer

The answer

To know if a sale makes money, take its price and subtract only the costs that sale causes. Costs that rise with each sale are variable costs; costs that stay put are fixed costs. What a sale leaves over is its contribution, and above zero means the sale helps.

Profit comes after contribution. All the contributions together, minus the fixed costs once, give the profit. Kiran's name for counting this way is marginal costing.

Contribution has a second name, the contribution margin. As a share of sales it is the contribution margin ratio; profit as a share of sales is the profit margin.

A percentage means nothing until you know what it is a percentage of. Profit over cost is markup, profit over price is margin, and on a profitable sale the margin is the smaller one.

The next unit costs only what it adds, which is its marginal cost. That can be less than the average cost (total cost divided by units), so one extra order can take a lower price than the whole batch.

What this unlocks next: how many sales you need before the month stops losing money, which is the jar's line divided by what each sale drops in ($12,000 ÷ $30 = 400 hoodies, called break-even), and then planning a whole year of spending around that number.

Question d · key item 4

Margin and profit: contribution margin = sales − variable costs; profit = contribution margin − fixed costs. March: $15,000 of margin, $3,000 of profit.

Question f · key item 6

Average and marginal cost: average = total cost ÷ units ($5,000 ÷ 100 = $50); marginal = the cost one more unit adds ($5,040 − $5,000 = $40).

Question g · key item 7

Margin and markup: markup = profit ÷ cost ($20 ÷ $80 = 25%); margin = profit ÷ price ($20 ÷ $100 = 20%).

Question h · key item 8

Break-even (the number of sales where the month stops losing money) = fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit. Fixed cost is what you pay at zero sales ($12,000); contribution per unit is price − variable cost ($50 − $20 = $30). Break-even is $12,000 ÷ $30 = 400 hoodies.

Worked example: the terms in break-even

Break-even is the number of units whose contributions just cover the fixed costs: fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit. A business has fixed costs of $9,000, sells at $40 and has a variable cost of $25. Explain each term and find break-even.

StepWorking
Fixed costpaid even at zero sales: $9,000
Contribution per unit$40 − $25 = $15, what each sale adds toward fixed costs
Break-even$9,000 ÷ $15 = 600 units

Below 600 units the business loses money. Each unit above 600 adds $15 of profit.
