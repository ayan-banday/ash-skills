# How every line is written

**Read break-into-field's writing rules first** (`../../break-into-field/references/writing-rules.md`): short paragraphs with bold lead-ins, every basic term explained, meaning first and the name after, the plain sentence before the metaphor, headings that say what the section tells you. Voice and banned words: `/write:match-ash-writing`. The rules below are the BBA book's own, from Ash's comments (the example page, Nov 1 2026; the Unit 2 pages, Nov 3 and 4). Each has a bad line and the fix. When his feedback changes one, rewrite that rule here; never add a numbered one.

## Every big heading is a founder's problem

**A chapter's `h2` and every problem section's `h3` is a problem a founder sees, never the answer posed as a question** (Ash, Nov 4 2026). One concept per heading, the running company named, written about the company (no "you", no "I" or "we"), with an object you can picture, and simple. The same goes for the founder questions at the end.
- Bad: "Why should I plan before I spend?" · "Where does Sensa's plan start, and how does one sales number set every other budget?" (the answer, posed as a question)
- Good: "Sensa expects to sell 300 sensors in January. How many must it build?" · "Sensa is building a noise-sensing model and a tool that halves assembly time. Why do they go in different budgets?" · "Sensa's February report shows twelve misses. Which two does it fix first?"
- Good, design thinking: "Tasklane built the dashboard teachers asked for. Why do they still grade on paper?"

## Every section stands alone

**When a section refers back, it names the earlier section and repeats the facts it needs.** Assume he read the earlier section and forgot the details; across chapter tabs, assume he never read the other chapter. A mistake is shown with real numbers and a real action. Ash: "you pretend that I will understand that because I remember the context."
- Bad: "Businesses with big fixed bills kept dropping products in slow months. Shared-out rent made every product look like a loser."
- Good: "**Back in 'What usually happens', the founders split April's $12,000 of rent across 300 hoodies.** That put $40 of rent on each $50 hoodie, so each looked like a $10 loss, and they stopped taking club orders. Each hoodie was really adding $30."

## Questions he answers

**Self-contained, one ask, open, never yes or no.** Start with what, why, how, which or how much. Read it with the rest of the book hidden: can he say what it asks? A practice ask names every thing to find.
- Bad: "Does this order make sense?"
- Good: "How much does this order add to or take from the month, and what is the lowest price you'd accept?"
- Bad "Your turn": "Find the same four things."
- Good: "Find the contribution per unit, the profit, the contribution margin ratio and the profit margin."

## The class's questions are taught, never listed

**A strong question the professor set becomes the worked example or "Your turn" of the section that teaches its concept, told in the chapter's story** (Ash, Nov 4 2026: "We don't want to have isolated knowledge"). Weak or repeat questions are dropped, and an assignment gets one line where its concept is taught.
- Bad: a closing section "The professor's class questions, answered", each card a definition.
- Good: inside the working-capital section, "Worked example: a fast-food restaurant owes more than it holds" with the professor's numbers, then "Your turn" with a cinema.

## Never a teacher's name, never where it came from

**Say "the professor", or nothing; never his or her name, on the page or in the tutor's config.** Never say where a line came from or how it relates to class: no "from your notes", "beyond class", "past what the professor taught", "the earlier version of this page" (Ash, Nov 4 2026: "I don't need to know this"). He wants the concept, why it matters to a founder, and how to use it.
- Bad: "This part goes past what the professor taught in class. It covers the cash budget from his shared notes."
- Good: "**Sensa's sales look fine, but salaries go out late.** Schools pay 60 days after delivery while the kit supplier wants cash now…"

## A metaphor only where the idea is hard

**Most sections need none.** When one is needed, pick a picture from daily life in a different world than the book's company, and follow it with a tiny example that stands on its own. Ash liked "a jar with a line painted on it at $12,000" and the chef who chops every onion before service (how indexing works).

## The idea box: a plain line, then a mini example

**The `.idea` box opens with one plain bold sentence a 7th grader could act on, then a mini example inside the box that works with nothing else read.**
- Bad: "For each sale, take away only the costs that sale itself causes from its price. What is left is what that sale adds towards rent and salaries." (Abstract, and no example.)
- Good: that sentence, then: "A $50 hoodie that costs $20 to make and ship adds $30. Sell 400 of them and you've covered $12,000 of rent."

## Two examples per concept, when the course isn't about tech

**Set each concept in the book's tech company, and for Managerial Accounting add a short factory example beside it** (`ash.md`). Ash: "a software-as-a-service company example would be helpful… managerial accounting… seems to be pretty geared towards the manufacturing sector."

## "Where you are now" is plain bullets

**One bullet per thing he has or lacks, in plain words; no "So what" line after it** (Ash, Nov 4 2026).
- Bad: "You know the break-even formula from the board. So what: you can get 250 units but can't yet use it to say no to a hire."
- Good: "You can get 250 units from the break-even formula, but not yet use it to decide on a hire."

## Also out in a BBA book

**No praise of the reader,** and none of: at the end of the day, when it comes to, in today's, demystify, optimize (match-ash-writing holds the rest of the list). No numbered section labels ("Question one") and no lead-in heading that only announces the sections below.

## The book and each chapter are named for the concept

**The book's `<title>`, its artifact name and each chapter's tab are the concept he's learning, in the course's words: "Variance Analysis", "Budgeting and Working Capital".** Never a clever line about the scenario. The founder problem is the heading; the name is what he'll search his gallery and calendar for. A book for a whole unit is named for the unit's concepts ("Budgeting and Variance Analysis"). Ash, Nov 3 2026: the titles were clever lines instead of the concept.
- Bad: "Why the Month Missed"
- Good: "Variance Analysis"
