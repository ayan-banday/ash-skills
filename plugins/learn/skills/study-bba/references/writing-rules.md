# How every line is written

These rules come from Ash's seven comments on the example page (Nov 1 2026), plus the writing rules he approved for Udyaan's coach and the break-into-field book. Each has the example page's bad line and the fix. The comments showed what he meant; the rules are what they add up to.

## 1. Every section stands alone

**When a section refers back, it names the earlier section and repeats the facts it needs.** Assume he read the earlier section and forgot the details. A mistake is shown with real numbers and a real action. Ash: "you pretend that I will understand that because I remember the context."
- Bad: "Businesses with big fixed bills kept dropping products in slow months. Shared-out rent made every product look like a loser."
- Good: "**Back in 'What usually happens', the founders split April's $12,000 of rent across 300 hoodies.** That put $40 of rent on each $50 hoodie, so each looked like a $10 loss, and they stopped taking club orders. Each hoodie was really adding $30."

## 2. Every heading, question and "Your turn" makes sense read alone

**Read it with the rest of the page hidden: can he say what it asks?** Name the things, the numbers and the situation inside it.
- Bad heading: "Which orders add less than nothing?"
- Good: "When does a big order make your month worse instead of better?"
- Bad "Your turn": "Find the same four things."
- Good: "Find the contribution per unit, the profit, the contribution margin ratio and the profit margin."

## 3. A metaphor only where the idea is hard

**Most sections need none.** When an idea is too hard to hold in plain words, use this order: the plain sentence, then a picture from daily life, then a tiny example that stands on its own, then the name. Ash liked "a jar with a line painted on it at $12,000" and the chef who chops every onion before service (how indexing works). Pick a picture from a different world than the page's company.

## 4. Meaning first, the name after

**Say what happens, what it produces, then "This is called X".** Every term gets its plain meaning beside it the first time it appears, even basic ones (margin, overhead, stakeholder).
- Bad: "Marginal cost is what one more unit adds."
- Good: "Making a 101st hoodie adds only its own $40, because the setup is already paid. What one more unit adds to total cost is called the marginal cost."

## 5. The idea box: a plain line, then a mini example

**The `.idea` box opens with one plain bold sentence a 7th grader could act on, then a mini example inside the box that works with nothing else read.**
- Bad: "For each sale, take away only the costs that sale itself causes from its price. What is left is what that sale adds towards rent and salaries." (Abstract, and no example.)
- Good: that sentence, then: "A $50 hoodie that costs $20 to make and ship adds $30. Sell 400 of them and you've covered $12,000 of rent."

## 6. Two examples per concept, when the course isn't about tech

**Set each concept in the page's tech company, and for Managerial Accounting add a short factory example beside it** (`ash.md`). Ash: "a software-as-a-service company example would be helpful… managerial accounting… seems to be pretty geared towards the manufacturing sector."

## 7. The voice

- **Spoken and warm, at a 7th-grade level.** Short sentences, plain verbs: pay, sell, keep, lose.
- **Paragraphs of 1 to 3 sentences and at most 60 words,** each with two or more sentences opening with a bold lead-in that states its point. Bullets: at most 2 sentences.
- **Say the claim.** No praise, no em dashes, no "not X but Y", no hedges.
- **Plain words over filler:** game changer, at the end of the day, when it comes to, in today's, dive in, journey, demystify, leverage (as a verb), empower, crucial, optimize, delve and embark all go.

## 8. Questions he answers

**Self-contained, one ask, open, never yes or no.** Start with what, why, how, which or how much. A practice ask names every thing to find.
- Bad: "Does this order make sense?"
- Good: "How much does this order add to or take from the month, and what is the lowest price you'd accept?"
