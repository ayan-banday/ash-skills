01 · From organisational psychology

How do you build a company culture where people move fast and own their work, before you've hired your first ten people?

Where you are now

  • You know what keeps people motivated: choice, people around them, and a sense of getting better at something. Those three needs tell you what people want, and they say nothing about how a team learns "how we do things here".
  • You plan to write the culture down: a pace, guardrails, a "what good looks like" brief per role. A written rule that people never see you live by could be ignored by week 3.
  • The culture part of the book is still ahead of you, so your first hires could arrive before you've picked which habits you'll reward.

After you read this, you can pick three habits for Udyaan's team and make sure every new person learns them in their first two weeks, without a speech.

What usually happens

WhenWhat happensWhat you see
Day 1The founder shares a values page: "We move fast. We own our work."Everyone nods
Week 2A designer shares a rough draft early; the founder lists three flaws in front of the teamNobody shares early again
Month 2Work arrives polished and late; the founder asks why everyone is slowCost: the team learned "polish first", the opposite of the page

Where the problem lies

The team copies what the founder rewards and punishes, and skips what the founder writes. Each person watches what happened the last time someone tried something, then repeats whatever was safe.

The idea that fixes it

Pick three habits. Then make each one win, every time, in four places: what you ask about each week, how you react when something breaks, who you praise, and who you hire or let go.

Picture a park. The architect paved neat paths, and a brown strip of worn earth cuts across the grass where people really walk. Nobody planned that strip: each walker followed the last one's steps until the shortcut became the path.

A team's habits form the same way, and the first steps they follow are yours. An MIT professor who spent his career on company culture listed the signals a team follows. He called them embedding mechanisms: the ways a leader's habits get built into a group.

How a habit becomes culture, from a first try to "how we do things here"

  1. Someone tries something, say, shares a rough draft on day 2. The whole team sees it.
  2. You react, and everyone reads the reaction. Thanks and one question make the try safe. A list of flaws in public makes it risky.
  3. The team repeats what was safe until nobody thinks about it. That unspoken rule is the culture, and the next new hire copies it on day one.

How you'd build it, and what changes for you

  • A habit list for you, never a poster: three habits, each with what it looks like on a Tuesday, like "a draft shared by day 2".
  • One weekly question you ask every person, which only makes sense if the habit is real.
  • Your first-minute reply to bad news, written before the bad news: "What did we learn, and what do we change?" The first crisis teaches more than any page.

What changes for you:

  • Your reactions become the training, and the ones on bad days count most.
  • Hiring becomes a culture tool: you hire for the three habits before the portfolio.

How it looks when you use it

The content designer asks: "The week 2 lesson is half done. Should I wait until it's polished?"

You answer, as you always do: "Send it now. Early beats perfect here. Which part are you unsure of?"

What comes back: a draft on Tuesday instead of a polished one on Friday, with three days left to fix the real problem.

What it cost: one sentence you've said ten times before.

Where else the same idea works

Far away: a car factory in California, 1984

  • GM's Fremont plant had one of the worst workforces in the company: wildcat strikes, sabotaged cars, and more than 1 in 5 workers absent on a normal day.
  • Toyota and GM reopened it as NUMMI with mostly the same workers. Toyota changed the daily work: any worker who saw a problem could stop the line, and a team leader came to help right away.
  • Absence fell to about 2%, and within a year quality went from GM's worst to GM's best, with the same people. A manager who helped set it up wrote that you change what people do first, and how they think follows.

In your world: Udyaan's onboarding team

  • Your three habits could be: show work early, say when you're stuck, check it with a real student.
  • Your weekly question: "What did a real student do with your work this week?"
  • The real-world tester's first bad report is your first crisis. Thank the tester in front of everyone, and every role learns that bad news is welcome.

When it isn't enough

  • Past about 20 people, you stop being the one everyone watches. Each person copies their own lead instead, so every lead needs your habit list and your weekly question. Org charts, rituals, stories and value statements then hold the habits in place; these are called secondary mechanisms, and they only work when the leaders already behave the same way.
  • Different teams can learn different habits under one roof. These pockets are called subcultures, and they matter most when two companies merge, which is the next article.
  • The test: after two weeks, could a new hire name your three habits without reading anything? If they'd quote the page instead, the culture lives on the page only.

The answer

You build a culture by picking three habits and making them win every time in what you ask about, how you react to bad news, who you praise and who you hire.

Those four signals are called embedding mechanisms, and the values page comes after, as a record of what already happens. NUMMI changed the same workers by changing their daily work first. A park's worn path forms the same way: people follow the steps in front of them.

02 · From corporate strategy

How do you buy or merge with another company without its culture breaking the value you paid for?

Where you are now

  • You've learned that a merger can solve more of a customer's problem, which is why it can raise what a company is worth.
  • You've learned that culture and day-to-day operations can break a merger, and you don't yet have a way to decide what to change and what to leave alone. On a first deal, that could mean guessing.
  • The guessing is common and expensive: a Harvard Business Review article cites studies putting acquisition failure between 70% and 90%.

After you read this, you can look at any deal, in the book or one of your own, and say which parts to join, which to leave alone, and why.

What usually happens

WhenWhat happensWhat you see
Day 1The buyer announces "one company, one way of working"Smiles at the party
Month 3The bought team's weekly releases now wait for the buyer's approvals; its best people start interviewingSlower launches
Year 2The best people are gone; the product customers loved goes staleCost: the buyer paid for something, then took it apart

Where the problem lies

The buyer joins everything by the same amount. Nobody asked which parts create the value and which parts have to stay the way they were to keep creating it.

The idea that fixes it

Before signing, ask two questions of each part of the company you're buying: must we share it to create the value, and would the value die if we changed it? Share the first kind and protect the second.

Two friends move into one flat. They share the kitchen and the wifi because sharing saves them money, and they keep their own bedrooms with doors that shut, because that's where each one sleeps well. Flatmates who knock down the bedroom walls to "be one household" start looking for a new flat.

Two strategy professors drew those two questions as a grid. Where a deal lands on the grid decides how much of it you join, and that choice is called the integration approach.

How it works, from a signed deal to one working company

  1. List where the value comes from, say, the bought company's customers, its product and its team's speed. The list is what you're paying for.
  2. Ask both questions of each item and place it on the grid. The customer list must be shared to sell them both products, and it won't die if moved: fold it in. The team's weekly releases needn't be shared and would die under your approvals: leave them alone.
  3. Join the shared parts first and guard the rest, and tell the bought team in writing which is which, so nobody has to guess what's safe.

How you'd build it, and what changes for you

  • A value list: one page naming the three to five things the deal is for.
  • The grid: every part of the bought company placed in one square, with one line on why.
  • A protection note: the bought team's leader writes down what must not change, and you sign it before the money moves.

What changes for you:

  • Fewer good people leave, because they know in writing what stays theirs.
  • Your price has a reason: you can point at each thing you're paying for.

How it looks when you use it

You ask: "Udyaan is buying a small app that teaches farmers to price their crops. What do we merge?"

The grid says: customers and payments fold in, so a farmer can buy the course and the app in one place. The app team's weekly releases and its support style stay theirs.

What comes back: a one-page plan the app's founder agrees to before signing.

What it cost: one afternoon, before any money moved.

Where else the same idea works

Far away: Germany, 1990

  • When East and West Germany joined in 1990, the East took the West's money, laws and institutions within months. The West German mark replaced the East's on 1 July 1990, and wages converted one to one.
  • It was the fullest fold-in a country could choose, with nothing left alone. Many Eastern factories couldn't survive at Western costs, and unemployment in the East rose fast in the early 1990s.
  • Germans even coined a word, Ostalgie, for missing parts of everyday East German life. The parts that made people feel at home were folded in along with the rest.

In your world: the micro-SaaS with Mohit

  • Say a two-person team with a tool your customers also use wants to join you.
  • Share the login and the billing, so a customer pays once for both.
  • Leave their support style alone if their customers praise it. That support style is part of what you'd be paying for.

When it isn't enough

  • When you buy a company to learn a new way of making money, even a little sharing can kill it. Christensen and colleagues say to keep that kind of company separate and learn from it, because your processes will bend it toward your old way.
  • The grid can't see the rules nobody says out loud, like whether juniors may disagree with the boss in a meeting. Buyers find those with a culture audit: interviews before signing about how decisions really get made.
  • The test: if you can't place each part of the deal in one square before you sign, you aren't ready to sign.

The answer

Decide part by part what must be shared to create the value and what would die if you changed it, then fold in the first and protect the second.

That choice is called the integration approach, and it turns "take care of culture" into a page you can write before signing. Flatmates do the same with kitchens and bedrooms. Germany in 1990 shows what folding in everything at once can cost.

03 · From factory floors and operating rooms

How do you learn a teammate's work is slipping on day 2 instead of day 10, without watching over their shoulder?

Where you are now

  • You plan a director-style team for Udyaan onboarding: you hold the vision, and a UI/UX person, a content designer and a real-world tester each get a "what good looks like" brief.
  • You plan to track progress where everyone can see it and flag people who aren't working. A flag aimed at people could teach them to hide problems until review day.
  • You're the final reviewer. If problems show up only at review, say day 10 of a 14-day sprint, you'd have 4 days to fix 10 days of drift.

After you read this, you can set up the team so the person who's stuck tells you first, on the same day.

What usually happens

WhenWhat happensWhat you see
Day 2The designer can't tell what "good" means for the sign-up screen, and guessesBoard says "in progress"
Day 6Guesses stack on guesses; the designer worries and says nothingBoard still says "in progress"
Day 10You review and the direction is wrongCost: 8 days redone, and you trust the board less

Where the problem lies

The person who first sees the problem gains nothing by saying so. A tracker that flags slow people makes speaking up riskier still, so the problem waits for you to find it.

The idea that fixes it

Give every person one easy way to say "I'm stuck" the moment it happens, and always answer the same way: thank them and help within the hour. Flag stuck work, never slow people.

On a city bus, any passenger can pull the cord, and the bus stops at the next stop. Nobody asks why you pulled it, and the driver never scolds you. Because pulling costs nothing, nobody rides past their stop.

Toyota's factories run on the same promise: a cord above the line that any worker pulls the moment something looks wrong, and a leader who comes to help. The cord and the light it switches on are called the andon.

How it works, from a stuck moment to a fixed one

  1. Someone hits a problem and pulls: one line in the team channel, "Stuck: sign-up screen, not sure what good looks like."
  2. You or a lead answer within the hour with help, and never ask why it took so long.
  3. The fix goes into the role's brief, so the next person doesn't hit the same wall. The board counts pulls each week; more pulls early means fewer surprises at review.

How you'd build it, and what changes for you

  • A cord: one fixed place and format for "stuck", like a message in the team channel that starts with "Stuck:".
  • A promise: your reply time, say within the hour during work hours, and the same first words every time: "Thanks for pulling."
  • A board that counts pulls and shows stuck work in one colour, without ranking people.

What changes for you:

  • You meet problems when they're a day old, while they're small.
  • Your day 10 review becomes a final polish, and your role as orchestrator is mostly answering pulls.

How it looks when you use it

The content designer posts: "Stuck: Day 4 lesson. The student example feels fake and I can't tell why."

You reply within 20 minutes: "Thanks for pulling. Use the tester's note from Monday: Riya couldn't work out what to charge for her pickles."

What comes back: a fixed lesson that afternoon.

What it cost: 20 minutes on day 2, instead of a rewrite on day 10.

Where else the same idea works

Far away: operating rooms in eight cities, 2007 to 2008

  • A World Health Organization team tested a 19-item surgical checklist in eight hospitals, New Delhi among them. Before the first cut, everyone in the room says their name and role.
  • The introductions are a cord. A junior nurse who has spoken once is more ready to speak again when something looks wrong, even in a room where the surgeon outranks everyone.
  • Deaths fell from 1.5% to 0.8%, and complications from 11% to 7%, across about 7,700 operations.

In your world: Fuji's build runs

  • A night run that can't finish a ready ticket could pull the cord: post "Stuck:" with the reason to the week page that night.
  • You'd answer it the next morning, instead of finding a half-done ticket at your review days later.

When it isn't enough

  • If people fear looking weak, nobody pulls, however easy the cord is. A team whose members believe they won't be punished for speaking up has psychological safety, and your first five replies to a pull build it or break it.
  • When you can't answer within the hour, people must decide alone. Armies send orders with the purpose and the end state attached, so soldiers can act when the plan breaks; that's called commander's intent. Your "what good looks like" brief becomes one when you add why it matters.
  • The test: count pulls each week. Zero pulls in a busy week means people are saving problems for review, so look at your replies first.

The answer

You learn on day 2 when the stuck person can say so for free and always gets thanks and help within the hour.

Toyota calls that cord the andon. Flag stuck work and never slow people, and your day 10 review turns into polish. Bus riders and surgical teams show the same thing: a free, safe signal finds problems faster than a watcher.
