## Day 1: What a Business Is

You read the welcome page, so you know the week fits around your classes.

Today you learn what a business is, and by Friday you'll use it to write your first offer.

## Starting with an idea leaves you stuck

Most people believe a business starts with an idea. So they:

- **Search for startup ideas** on YouTube and Instagram
- **Ask classmates if the idea is good**, and hear polite yeses
- **Pick a name and a logo** before anyone has asked for anything
- **Lie awake** wondering if they should build it at all

A few months later they either never started or built something nobody uses.

## A problem needs a goal

A business starts with what a person wants. For example:

> **Goal:** You're hungry, so you want to eat
> **Problem:** You have no snacks
> **Solution:** You buy snacks from a vending machine

You wouldn't buy snacks if you weren't hungry. **A problem needs a goal**, and without one there is nothing to be stuck on.

An idea is the solution line. It only sells when a real goal and problem sit above it.

## A business removes the blocker and gets paid for it

A business is **people trying to reach a goal, and something stopping them.**

- **The people** are the customers
- **The goal** is where they want to end up
- **The blocker** is the problem
- **The business** removes the blocker, and the people pay for that

## No goal behind the blocker means no customer

Take a home baker with 30 regular orders. She wants to expand to three new areas, but she can't find delivery drivers. She'd pay for a delivery service tomorrow.

A second baker has the same orders and the same driver problem. She likes staying small and baking for her regulars. She won't pay for anything, because the blocker isn't in her way.

A delivery service built for the second baker has no customer.

## Fix the blocker that stops the goal hardest

A business usually has several blockers. **Fix the one that stops the goal hardest.**

To test it, ask: "If this blocker disappeared tomorrow, how much closer would they be?"

Take a company that packs fruit pulp for cafes in one city and wants to enter two new cities in two years. Three blockers stand in its way:

- **Fruit spoils** on the way from the farms to the factory
- **Two delivery drivers** just quit
- **One supplier** keeps delivering late

**Good pick: spoilage.** The company can't open two new cities if a fifth of its fruit rots before it's packed.

**Bad pick: the late supplier.** Every delivery could arrive on time and a fifth of the fruit would still rot.

**Bad pick: the drivers.** New drivers would carry the same spoiled fruit to the same city.

## Day 2: What Agricultural Businesses Look Like

Yesterday you found the goal and the biggest blocker in a case, and saved it to your Day 1 doc.

Today you find where people with goals and blockers live in agriculture, and where you could fit.

## Thinking agriculture means farming rules you out

If you study computer science, design, or law, you may think agriculture has no room for you. So you:

- **Rule yourself out** because you don't own land and have never farmed
- **Picture only farmers and crop traders**, so every idea you come up with sits in those two spots
- **Grab the first idea** that shows up, because you can't see the rest of the field

Most of the field stays invisible to you, including the parts that fit your degree.

## Every hand a banana passes through is a possible customer

Think of a banana you buy at the canteen. Someone grew it, someone collected it from many small farms, someone ripened it, someone drove it to the city, and someone sold it to you.

Each of those people has a goal and a blocker, so each one is a possible customer. A business can start at any of those hands.

## The chain has eight links

- **Inputs:** seeds, fertiliser, tools, loans
- **Growing:** raising crops and animals
- **Collecting:** gathering from many small farms
- **Processing:** cleaning, sorting, packing
- **Moving and storing:** trucks, cold rooms, warehouses
- **Selling:** shops, markets, canteens
- **Services:** advice, insurance, training
- **Technology:** software, data, sensors

You don't need to know each one in depth. The GPT holds the detail.

## Your degree decides where you start

- **Computer science:** software and data tools for any link, such as tracking a harvest or matching sellers to buyers
- **Business:** pricing, selling, collecting from many small farms, and money
- **Other degrees:** you know something nobody else in the chain does, and the GPT can help place it

Already have a problem in mind? Find the link it sits in. The person who has it lives in that link.

## Pick the link you can reach this week

**Pick a link where you can reach people this week.**

**Good pick: selling.** A student whose family runs a vegetable shop can talk to a seller by tonight.

**Bad pick: exporting.** It sounds big, but the student doesn't know one exporter, so nobody can answer their questions.

## Day 3: Is This Problem Worth Solving?

Yesterday you picked a link in the chain and a person who lives there.

Today you check whether that person's problem is worth your time.

## Seeing a problem once is a weak reason to build

Say you notice the canteen queue is long every day, so you spend three weeks building a pre-order app. Then you ask the people in the queue, and most of them say they chat with friends while they wait and don't mind.

The queue annoyed you and nobody else, so you built an app for a problem nobody had.

## A problem worth solving fits in one sentence

A problem worth solving belongs to a specific group, comes up often, and costs them something. Write it in one line:

> **[Who]** want **[goal]**, but **[blocker]** when **[situation]**.

For example: "Small vegetable farmers near one town want a fair price, but they wait days for a buyer once the harvest is ready."

If any bracket is vague, the problem is vague.

**Name a group you could find this week.**

**Good: "Small vegetable farmers near one town."** You could look one up.

**Bad: "Farmers."** There are millions of them, and you don't know where to start.

## Four questions test it

- **Who exactly?** Could you describe them well enough to find one?
- **How often does it hit them?** Every week, or once in a few years?
- **What do they do about it today?** Even a bad workaround counts.
- **What does that cost them?** Money, time, spoiled food.

For the farmers: they sell to the first buyer who shows up, at a lower price, and a few days of waiting means some crop spoils.

**Pick small and weekly over big and rare.**

**Good: a vegetable seller loses a little stock to spoilage every week.** You can ask one next week.

**Bad: "Climate change hurts farmers."** It's big, but nobody you can reach can tell you what to build.

## You haven't talked to anyone yet, so most answers are guesses

Mark each answer as **Known** if you saw it or lived it, and **Guess** if you believe it but haven't checked.

Is a guess good enough? Today, yes. Next week you check it with real people.

## What these three show

- **Different openers on purpose.** Day 1 uses the common belief. Day 2 speaks to the student's own doubt. Day 3 opens with a short scene.
- **The same skeleton under each.** Bridge, opener with impact, truth, examples, rule with good and bad picks, the Do, week map, tomorrow.
- **Mistakes sit inside examples and rules.** No module has a "mistakes" section.
- **Objections sit where they arise.** Day 1 handles "I already have an idea" in one sentence under the snack example. Day 2 handles "I already have a problem" under the degree bullets.
- **The Do is always explain, link, save.** The GPT holds the case, the interview, and the summary.
