# Good and Bad

Every pair below comes from a real correction by Ash. Read the whole file before drafting. The test behind every pair is the same: **does the sentence add something, and would a 7th grader get it the first time?**

Contents: sentence patterns, headings and bridges, bullets and openers, examples, objections, the Do, formatting, found in testing, found in round 2, and the banned-pattern scan list.

## Sentence patterns

**1. Lead-ins that announce the next thing**

Bad:
> Here's what it looks like for a student:

Good:
> For example:

Why: the lead-in is filler. The reader sees the example anyway. "Here's what..." also reads as AI writing, which Ash flags every time.

**2. Counting out the choices**

Bad:
> A few months later, one of two things has happened. They never started, or they built something that nobody uses.

Good:
> A few months later they either never started or built something nobody uses.

Why: the setup sentence ("one of two things") does no work. Say the thing once.

**3. Sentences that comment on the page**

Bad:
> All three are real. The one that matters most is the one that blocks the goal hardest, and that's spoilage.

Good:
> The biggest problem is spoilage. A company can't open two new cities if a fifth of its fruit rots before it's packed.

Why: "All three are real" tells the reader nothing. The good version gives the answer and its impact together.

**4. Labeling sentences**

Bad:
> Both groups make the same error.

Good: delete it.

Why: it names a pattern instead of showing one. The bullets above it already show it.

**5. Abstract explanation where a picture would do**

Bad:
> An idea is a guess at a solution, so if you start there you spend your time defending the guess and never look at the people it's for.

Good:
> - **Search for startup ideas** on YouTube and Instagram
> - **Ask classmates if the idea is good**, and hear polite yeses

Why: the bad version is true and unreadable. The good version lists what the student does.

**6. Negative parallelism**

Bad:
> This isn't about having a big idea. It's about spotting a problem.

Good:
> Businesses usually start when someone notices a problem that keeps annoying a group of people.

**7. Fragments and one-word sentences**

Bad:
> No farm needed. No plan needed. Just a problem.

Good:
> You don't need a farm or a plan to start.

**8. Matched triplets and even rhythm**

Bad:
> You will learn what matters. You will test what works. You will leave with a plan.

Good:
> You'll learn how to spot a problem worth solving, test it with a few real people, and leave knowing what to do next.

**9. Em dashes**

Bad: "The fruit spoils — and the company loses the city."
Good: "The fruit spoils, and the company loses the city."

**10. Impact held back**

Bad:
> Spoilage is the one that matters. It's worth thinking about why.

Good:
> The biggest problem is spoilage. A company can't open two new cities if a fifth of its fruit rots before it's packed.

## Headings and bridges

**Headings that name a slot or a label**

Bad: "Why start here", "Mistakes to avoid", "Key takeaways", "Five kinds of places hold your customers' words"
Good: "Where do I find what farmers say?", "What do I type into the search bar?", "What do I ask the GPT?"

A heading is the question the student would ask next at that point (Ash, October 2026). The section under it answers that question in full. A reader scanning the headings alone sees the path through the day.

**Headings that sound clever**

**Clever headings fail.** A heading passes when someone who hasn't read the section knows exactly what it covers: a plain question the student would ask.

- Bad: "Two pictures to think with, and three rules that hold everywhere". It counts the contents and hides them.
- Bad: "Three rules, two pictures and two questions to carry into your own tests"
- Bad: "Symmetry is the language of trust". An aphorism makes the reader decode it.
- Good: "How do you know people will pay for your SaaS before you build it?" A question the reader would ask.
- **Why:** a clever heading is a puzzle the reader solves before reading. A plain one tells them where they are.

**Test:** read the heading alone. If you can't say what the section will tell you, rewrite it as the plainer question.

**Bridges that restate the schedule**

Bad:
> You read the welcome page, so you know the six weeks fit around your week.

Good (Day 1):
> You read the welcome page, so you know the week fits around your classes.

Good (later days):
> Yesterday you found the goal and the biggest blocker in a case, and saved it to your Day 1 doc.

The bridge names something the student made, so it proves the course remembers them.

## Bullets and openers

**Generic behaviors**

Bad:
> - Research how to build the idea
> - Get confused if they should build it

Good:
> - **Search for startup ideas** on YouTube and Instagram
> - **Ask classmates if the idea is good**, and hear polite yeses
> - **Pick a name and a logo** before anyone has asked for anything

Use the places, apps, and habits of the reader. If you cannot name a real behavior, you do not know the reader well enough yet. Go back to the spec.

**Open the same way every day**

Bad: Day 1, Day 2, and Day 3 each start with "Most people believe".
Good: Day 1 uses "Most people believe". Day 2 speaks to the student ("If you study computer science, you may think..."). Day 3 opens with a short scene. See `variation.md`.

## Examples

**Examples too complex for the lesson**

Bad:
> You want to cut your study time before exams (goal). You have no notes (problem). A senior sells you notes (solution). You won't pay for the notes if you had already studied.

Good:
> **Goal:** You're hungry, so you want to eat
> **Problem:** You have no snacks
> **Solution:** You buy snacks from a vending machine

Why: three lines, one idea, no setup. The reader needs no context about exams or seniors.

**Labels that apologize**

Bad:
> (Made up, but you've probably felt it.)
> Here's a made-up company. (We'll swap in a real, verified one later.)

Good: write the example plainly. Invented details are fine. Fake statistics and real company claims are out.

**Bridging back to an earlier example for no reason**

Bad:
> Back to the snacks. The vending machine is a business because many hungry people have the same blocker.

Good: move to the baker, which is the clearer, more specific case, and let the snack example stay where it taught its point.

**A rule with no test and no examples**

Bad:
> Solve the biggest problem first.

Good:
> **Fix the blocker that stops the goal hardest.**
> To test it, ask: "If this blocker disappeared tomorrow, how much closer would they be?"
> **Good pick: spoilage.** The company can't open two new cities if a fifth of its fruit rots before it's packed.
> **Bad pick: the late supplier.** Every delivery could arrive on time and a fifth of the fruit would still rot.

## Objections

**Objections as a pasted aside**

Bad:
> (Already have an idea? Keep it. On Wednesday you'll check whether it solves a real problem.)

Good (placed where the claim arises, in the same breath):
> An idea is the solution line. It only sells when a real goal and problem sit above it.

**A generic FAQ block**

Bad:
> - "What if I run short on time?" Do step 3 and catch up on the weekend.
> - "What if the GPT and I disagree?" Good. That's the practice.

Good: delete the block. Keep an objection only if this day's specific claim raises it.

**A forced question at the end of every section**

Bad: every section ends on "So where does your idea fit?", "Does every blocker become a customer?", "Which one do you pick?"
Good: use a question only when a real reader would ask it. Most sections just follow the previous one.

## The Do

**Too much on the page**

Bad:
> Here is a short case: [six lines of company details]
> 1. Open the course GPT and paste this: [prompt]
> 2. Disagree with the GPT at least once.
> 3. Open W1.1 in your Workbook and write two lines about you.
> What a finished version looks like: [block]

Good:
> Open the [course GPT](#) and type "Day 1". It gives you a short case and interviews you one question at a time about the goal, the blockers, and which one matters most.
> Disagree with it at least once and defend your answer.
> When it finishes, it gives you a summary. Save it in a Google Doc called "Day 1: Goal and Blocker".
> **Short on time?** Ask the GPT to interview you about your own semester instead.

Why: the GPT holds the case and the interview. The page explains, links, and names the doc.

## Formatting

**Over-bolding**

Bad: **You should write down your problem and then think about who else has it.**
Good: Write down your **problem**, then write **who else has it**.

**Paragraph where bullets belong**

Bad:
> Your week is already full. You have exams to study for, projects due, and friends and family who want your time.

Good:
> **Your week is already full:**
> - **Exams** you need to study for
> - **Projects and assignments** with deadlines
> - **Friends and family** who want your time

**Pop-culture detours**

Bad: a Tony Stark paragraph mid-lesson, added for flavor.
Good: leave it out unless Ash asks for one. It spent a section of words on a metaphor the student did not need.

## Found in testing

These came from a fresh agent's first drafts of Days 4 and 5.

**Chatter before the title**

Bad: the reply opens "Now I'll draft Day 4." and the line gets pasted into the page.
Good: the reply starts with `# Day 4: What First-Time Founders Get Wrong`.

**An invented frequency**

Bad: "Most people make two or three of these at once."
Good: "Check your own problem against each one." Say what to do, not how common it is.

**A reused example**

Bad: Day 4 retells the canteen queue and the pre-order app from Day 3.
Good: a new case for Day 4, such as a student who builds a seed-tracking app for a farm they never visited.

**A heading that names the scene**

Bad: "A second-year student notices the canteen snack line moves slowly"
Good: "What happens if I build before I ask anyone?"

**Opener bullets that name no behavior**

Bad: "Trust your own judgment without asking anyone else"
Good: "Message three classmates and treat a thumbs-up as proof"

**An example that fails the lesson**

Bad: an offer with the line "They pay: No fee. The buyer and farmer negotiate the price". The student's offer has no payer, which is the thing Day 5 teaches.
Good: "They pay: Rs 40 per kg of produce collected, taken from the buyer." The line names who pays and how much.

**A miscounted list**

Bad: "Four mistakes at once: product love, everyone, opinions, building early, and no cost model."
Good: "Five mistakes at once" when you list five, and the same count everywhere.

**Borrowing a test that belongs to another rule**

Bad: Day 5 reuses Day 1's blocker test ("If this blocker disappeared...") for an offer rule.
Good: a test that fits the rule, such as "Could a stranger read this line and know who to call tomorrow?"

**Skill terms in the judgment calls**

Bad: "Opener type: used 'cost first' per the rotation plan."
Good: "The opener starts from the cost of skipping the check, since Day 3 opened with a scene."

**Listing the same items twice**

Bad: the five mistakes appear as bullets in the opener, then again under "The five mistakes that trap builders".
Good: one list, in the opener, with a concrete behavior on each line. Later sections work on one item, not the whole list.

**A heading that claims a frequency**

Bad: "Most builders make the exact same mistakes"
Good: "Why check my problem before I build?"

**Negative parallelism with a comma**

Bad: "They are patterns in how founders think, not signs of carelessness."
Good: "They are patterns in how founders think."

**Jargon carried over from the plan**

Bad: "The GPT red-teams your problem."
Good: "The GPT pokes holes in your problem, one mistake at a time."

**A tick-mark checklist before the title**

Bad: the reply opens "Perfect. The linter passes: ✓ Every sentence adds something ✓ ..." and then the module.
Good: the reply opens with `# Day 4: ...`. Process notes, if any, go after the judgment calls.

## Found in round 2 (October 2026)

These came from Ash's feedback on the first drafts of Weeks 2 to 6, and from the fresh reviewers of the rewrite.

**A question that uses a word the student hasn't learned**

Bad: "How do I find out if people want my offer?" (Week 2, when "offer" was taught only on Day 5 and still sounds like business jargon)
Good: "How do I find out if people want my solution, before I spend money on it?"

**A phrase that makes no sense on its own**

Bad: "farmers night current motor", "farmer pump night electricity"
Good: "why farmers water their fields at night"
Why: a newcomer can't decode the first two. The good one teaches the fact (power comes only at night) as you read it.

**A bridge that only says what's next**

Bad:
> Last week you wrote Offer v1. Today you find where your customers talk online.

Good:
> Till now you have five tabs in your Offer Document. Every one of those five lines is still **your guess**, because nobody who has the problem has read it.
> Say you spend a month and **₹15,000** building an app, and the farmers say the seed shop owner already tells them, free.

Why: the day solves the problem yesterday left. Name the problem, and what ignoring it costs.

**A step that asks the student to read**

Bad: "Read the comments under five videos and note what farmers complain about."
Good: "**Select everything, copy it, and paste it raw.** Don't read it, sort it or tidy it. The GPT does the sorting."

**A GPT question with no problem in it**

Bad: "Ask the GPT to summarise your notes."
Good:
> What do farmers say about **[your problem]**? And what do agri entrepreneurs say about it?

Why: the problem tells the GPT what to look for. Without it, the GPT sums up the ads and menus too.

**A teach-back section on the page**

Bad: a section "Explaining it back counts most" telling the student the GPT will ask them to explain the idea in their own words.
Good: the GPT records the evidence in the doc; the page spends its words on the day.

**Re-introducing the running student**

Bad: Day 6 opens a section with Gia's bio and the drawing of her uncle's 2 a.m. walk, already on the welcome page.
Good: "On Day 4 Gia's fix was to hear from at least three more farmers who make the night walk. This is the Offer v1 she's checking:"

**An example that breaks the day's own rule**

Bad: Day 3 teaches "[Who] want [goal], but [blocker] when [situation]", and Gia's sentence ends ", so they walk to the field in the dark".
Good: "Small borewell farmers near Kunigal want **to water their crops on time**, but **they have to walk to the field to switch on the pump** **when the power comes at night**."

Bad: Day 22 teaches "ask about a real time, in a way they could answer no", and Gia's message starts "I think motors burn when the power flickers. Is that right?"
Good: "The last three burnt motors you repaired, what was the power like the night before, and was the farmer at the pump?"

Bad: Day 1's blocker is "power comes only at night", which no business can remove.
Good: "He has to be at the field to switch the pump on when the power comes at 2 a.m."

**A source read the wrong way**

Bad: "Farmers spend ₹10,000 to ₹12,000 a year on repairs," quoting a farmer who, the same article says, bought a ₹400 switch and stopped paying for repairs.
Good: "₹10,000 to ₹12,000 a year, **before he bought a ₹400 switch**." Then the surprise: two of the farmers she quoted already fixed it.

**One kind of customer only**

Bad: every place to search, every example and the Do are about farmers.
Good: each also says how it works when your customer is an agri entrepreneur: "If your customer is a cold room owner, try 'why cold room owners lose vegetables in summer'."

**Brochure lines**

Bad: "Udyaan has your back." / "The farmer's words do the heavy lifting." / "It trains evidence seeking at its hardest."
Good: "Udyaan helps you through it." / "The farmer's words matter most." / "It trains evidence seeking: changing your mind when a real person's answer says so."

**A word used before it's explained**

Bad: "Raju rewound three motors last week."
Good: "Raju rewound three motors last week. Rewinding is the repair for a burnt motor: the mechanic replaces its burnt copper wire."

**A question left open**

Bad: "Click 'Show transcript' and copy it." (Many Kannada videos have no transcript button, and the student stops there.)
Good: add "No 'Show transcript' button? Many Kannada videos don't have one. Copy the comments only."

## Banned-pattern scan

Search the draft for these before sending.

- `Here's` / `Here is what` / `Here are`
- `one of two` / `a few things` / `two things`
- `not .* but` / `isn't .* it's` / `It's not`
- `Let's dive` / `It's worth noting` / `Now you try it`
- `(made up` / `(Made up` / `(We'll swap`
- `—` (em dash)
- Any single-word sentence ending in a period
- Any paragraph over three sentences
- Any heading that names a slot or a label instead of asking the student's question
- `Your week, day by day` (the week map belongs to the page design)
- `explain it back` / `in your own words` as a section on the page
