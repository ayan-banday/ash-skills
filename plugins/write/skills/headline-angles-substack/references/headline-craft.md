# Headline Craft

The rules every headline and subhead pair must follow, whichever of the 10 ways it comes from.

## The clarity test (overrides everything else)

Imagine a total stranger who has never heard of this newsletter, knows nothing about the topic, and is scrolling past it in 2 seconds. Read only the headline and subhead. Can that stranger say, in 1 sentence, exactly what they would learn or get by reading it?

If the answer is fuzzy or requires already knowing the framework being named, the pair fails, however clever it is. When in doubt, run the test again before showing anything.

**Failed:**
Headline: The Convergence Problem
Subhead: You are not scattered. You are early.
A stranger has no idea what the convergence problem is or what they would learn. "Early" to what? It sounds insightful and says nothing.

**Passed:**
Headline: How I Combine My Scattered Interests Into One Meaningful, Profitable Calling
Subhead: In a world that punishes you for being creative.

**Passed:**
Headline: The 6 Parts Every Actionable Nonfiction Book Repeats
Subhead: Once you see the pattern, writing becomes a fill-in-the-blanks exercise.

## Inventory first

Before writing, note what the material honestly contains:
- a number (a count of steps, parts, reasons, years, dollars)
- a personal transformation, with before and after numbers
- a named structure or framework
- a stake: what the reader gains, or what not knowing costs them
- whether the piece is original or adapted from someone else's work

When the material has real versions of these, use them and drop any [invented] tag. When it does not, invent them under the invention rule in SKILL.md.

## Two modes, mixed across the set

A set of pairs mixes modes based on what each way needs. Do not force every pair into the same shape.

### Mode A: Personal Narrative

Use for Personal story, Lessons, and any pair told from lived experience. "I" leads the headline, and numbers go straight into the headline as proof.

- **Good:** "I Used to Take 4 Years to Write a Book. Now It Takes 14 Days."
- **Good:** "First $1 Roadmap: How I'd Make Money Online If I Was Starting Over (After Generating $30,000,000)"
- **Bad:** "I Figured Out the One Trick to Better Focus." It has "I" and no story or number. First person with no proof is a claim wearing a costume. Add a before and after number, or switch to Mode B.

### Mode B: Five-Piece Framework

Use for every way that is not a personal story. Draw from these pieces and keep only the ones that earn a place:

1. **Number:** how many things you promise, in numerals
2. **What:** the literal subject, plain enough to get in 1 pass
3. **Who:** the reader, named or implied by situation
4. **Why:** the stake, a cost paid or a benefit gained, never a guarantee
5. **Twist:** a second stake stacked on the first, in parentheses, only if it is true

- **Good:** "The 6 Parts Every Actionable Nonfiction Book Repeats"
- **Good:** "The 10 Shifts That Decide Who Survives the AI Economy (and Why Being Good at AI Isn't One of Them)"
- **Bad:** "5 Reasons Writing a Book Takes So Long" when the material names only 2 reasons. When real material exists, the number must match it.

### Never use a third mode

A coined concept name plus an aphorism ("The Convergence Problem" / "You are early") fails the clarity test almost automatically, because a coined term needs its own explanation and a headline has no room for one. Route philosophical material through Mode B: name the actual structure or claim, never an invented label for it.

## Name the reader by situation

Speak to what the reader does or faces: "founders with 2 to 6 clients and a full week", "a student who reads every day and has shipped nothing". Labels like "smart people", "high performers", or "creatives" fail the swap test because they fit every piece. Where the reader studies or works never defines them.

## Numerals, always

Every number in a headline or subhead is a numeral, however small.

- **Wrong:** "Two Things You Need to Ghostwrite in Any Industry"
- **Right:** "The 2 Things You Need to Ghostwrite in Any Industry"

## The subhead

The subhead says what the headline had no room for. It never restates the headline.

- **Bad:** Headline "3 Reasons Your Focus Routine Keeps Failing" with subhead "3 reasons your focus routine keeps failing and what to do." It adds zero information.

**Length is a feel.** Real winning subheads run from 3 words to 18 words. Aim for roughly 6 to 16 and never pad or cut to hit a number. The job decides the length.

**Proof can be yours or someone else's.** A first-person headline needs no first-person subhead. A Mode B headline can take third-party proof or method-as-proof:
- "How to Build a Tiny AI Company That Actually Makes Money" with "And how one guy uses the same playbook to make $3 million per day."
- "How to Find a Business Hiding in the Past" with "8 steps to spot opportunities everyone else overlooks."

**Give each subhead at least 1 of these 4 jobs, ideally 2:**
1. **Who, by situation:** names the reader through what they do or feel. Example: "If you overthink what to write about."
2. **Proof or method:** a mechanism, a technique count, or a specific result. Example: "The 6-part pattern that made the blank page stop being a problem."
3. **Named contents:** what is inside, stated as a list. Example: "25 business models ranked for starting, scaling, surviving AI pressure test reality."
4. **Objection or gap:** the reader's likely pushback, or a gap in what they believe. Example: "Why just taking action will never work for you."

## Cut list

Cut these on sight.

- **Negative parallelism.** Never "not X, but Y" or "isn't A, it's B."
  Bad: "It's not about discipline, it's about design." Good: "Discipline is a byproduct of design."
- **Hedging openers.** Never announce the headline is coming.
  Bad: "Here's the thing about focus routines." Good: "Your focus routine was never the problem."
- **Guarantee language.** No promises of certainty.
  Bad: "The routine that guarantees you'll never procrastinate again." Good: "3 Reasons Your Focus Routine Keeps Failing."
- **Avoid words:** hustle, authentic, journey, leverage, impact, game-changer, supercharge, delve, empower, utilize, crucial, optimize.
- **Ungrounded abstraction.** If a subhead could sit on 10 unrelated pieces, anchor it to a number, a named thing, or a real cost.
  Bad: "A new way to think about what really matters in your work." Good: "Routine, energy, and meaning, ranked by what each one costs you to keep."
- **Dangling referents.** "This", "that", or "it" needs a clear antecedent inside the same sentence.
  Bad: "This Changes Everything About How You Write." Good: "This 6-Part Structure Changes How You Write a Book."
- **Coined concept names** used as the headline (see "Never use a third mode").
- **Spelled-out numbers.**
