## 1. The Language of Visual Storytelling (Cut Your Mustard)

What is visual storytelling, and how do great filmmakers convey such complex ideas and emotions without dialogue?
For over a hundred years our eyes have been fixed on the screen: in cinemas, in our homes, on our devices. The
simplest and most elegant form of cinema is visual storytelling: show, don't tell. At first it was the only way to
tell a story, until Al Jolson spoke on screen for the first time. Having no dialogue can still be just as effective
today: *The Artist*, the opening of *WALL-E*, *There Will Be Blood*, *2001: A Space Odyssey*, the showdown in *The
Silence of the Lambs*, the finale of *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly*, the listening-booth scene in *Before Sunrise*.

**The married-life montage in Pixar's *Up*.** The journey of a married couple, their dreams, life, love, loss and
eventual end, told without words in just over four and a half minutes.

- **Character through contrast.** Watch the wedding with the sound off and you still see a young couple, and more:
  their backgrounds. One side of the church is loud and cheerful, the other plain and quiet, just like the two of
  them.
- **Familiar concepts everyone understands.** A simple family, a new home. We quickly see their jobs.
- **Dreams shown, not said.** A cloud becomes a child: we see their next plan in life.
- **Something complex in two shots.** The nursery is decorated; then music, colour and a lateral tracking shot,
  a poster, a doctor and body language tell us a child is not possible. "They say a picture is worth a thousand
  words."
- **A new goal.** Times get hard, so they find something new to look forward to: the adventure.
- **A connective thread that is also a measure.** The savings jar runs through the whole sequence and stands for
  their progress. Life breaks it open with literal bumps in the road: a flat tyre, a broken leg, a damaged roof.
  Through each setback they stay happy together.
- **Time shown by one repeated object.** As time speeds up, Carl's tie, knotted again and again, shows the years
  passing.
- **The payoff.** After so many years Carl remembers the dream and buys the tickets, but it is too late. The man who
  always tried to keep up with his adventurous wife has seen time slow her down.
- **Full circle.** It ends with Carl alone in the church where it began. Using the same place for the first and last
  scene tells the audience, if only subconsciously, that the story is over, at least for now.

**The challenge they set.** A 30 to 60 second video, no dialogue, the first and last scene in the same place, on the
theme of waiting.

## 2. Visual Storytelling 101 (Film Riot, Philip Bloom)

Covers what we film: how we arrange the subject in the frame, the shot language, how lenses change the image, and
how light and contrast play a role. The subject matters, but a pleasing image comes from how it is built in the
frame. Composition works in film the same way it does in painting, illustration and graphic design: it arranges
shapes so they please us, and it guides the eye to what we want it to see, or not see.

- **Rule of thirds.** Divide the frame into thirds both ways and put the things of interest on the crossing points.
  In a wide shot it balances the subject against the surroundings; in a medium shot the person sits on a line; in a
  close-up the eyes go on the crossing points. "The eyes are the windows to the soul." Keep the eyes there and in
  focus and you can get away with a lot, even cutting off the top of the head. Know the rule so you know when to
  break it.
- **Central framing.** A subject in the dead centre draws attention to itself. Good for fast cutting, because the
  eye does not have to travel to find what matters.
- **One-point perspective.** Central framing with leading lines and symmetry (Kubrick, Wes Anderson) draws you into
  the frame and fakes depth.
- **Leading lines.** Lines in the frame direct the eye to the main point of interest.
- **Depth of field.** A shallow focus marks the subject, and their inner story, as what matters.
- **Scale is importance.** Filling the frame (an extreme close-up) tells you how important a person is. Making them
  small in a wide shot shows how they feel, or what they seem worth.
- **Light and contrast.** "We are naturally pulled towards looking at brighter objects in the frame." A dark frame
  with one lit spot pulls you right in.
- **A frame within the frame.** A doorway or window frames the subject.
- **Lead space.** A character looking left sits on the right, looking into the empty space. Breaking it is a choice.
- **Shot sizes, wide to tight.** Extreme wide and wide (an 18mm on full frame) establish the place and a sense of
  space. Full shot (head to toe). Medium long (knees up; never cut at the ankles). Cowboy (mid-thigh). Medium
  (50mm, what the eye sees). Medium close-up (interviews, dialogue). Close-up ("the choker"). Extreme close-up (eyes
  and mouth, for heightened connection). Macro (so close you see the camera in her eye).
- **Lenses change the background.** Keep the subject the same size and lengthen the lens: the background narrows,
  blurs and is pulled closer, until at 300 to 400mm two far-apart things look near each other (kids on Brighton
  Beach who seem about to be hit by a wave, but are safe). Only focal length does this.
- **Lenses change the face.** 50 to 90mm (around 85mm) looks like the person really does. Wider lenses widen the face
  and enlarge the nose; very wide is "just completely bizarre".
- Composition becomes second nature: "I go to a restaurant and choose a table based on a nice background and frame."

## 3. Give Me 13 Minutes (By Almén)

A breakdown of his most-loved video, which opens with a friend, Chris, asking: "What would have had to happen in a
week for you to look back on that week with pride?"

- **The intro does three things.** It gives context (what the story is about). It creates a question: "if there is
  nothing your audience wants to know or is waiting for, there is a high chance they skip." It creates emotion,
  because without emotion it is very hard to get engaged with anything. The question "what would make you proud of a
  week?" works because everyone can resonate with it, and now you are, almost subconsciously, waiting for the answer.
- **Where you start can make or break the story.** He could have opened on his desk with "YouTube is hard". Then you
  think the video is about YouTube. Opening on Chris's question is far more interesting. Same story, different first
  scene.
- **Sound for feelings you cannot film.** The idea "actually sucks": a riser builds tension and then everything cuts
  dead, like hitting a brick wall. Later, a low rumble builds a bubble of anxiety to put you inside his head. "If you
  can't film it, try to create it with sound."
- **The tension pattern.** Draw the timeline against tension. The question raises tension; the title releases it; he
  plans and scripts and tension rises; the idea fails and tension drops to the bottom; the mood picks up and climbs
  back. "This hook and release pattern is one of the key things I learned about storytelling." Without it, it is very
  hard to keep an audience engaged, on any platform.
- **Vary the rhythm.** If an edit drags, add a few quick shots after the long ones. Cut fast through what is not
  crucial (the soda, the popcorn). Hold long on what is (his stuckness and disappointment).
- **Two layers.** His early stories had one layer, the goal: a character going from A to B. Studying travel films and
  vlogs, he saw why they all have voice-over: it adds a second layer, the emotions and the inner journey. What you
  see on screen is the physical journey; the voice carries the mental one.
- **Metaphor in the second layer.** His houseplants are dying through the video, because the video is not about
  making YouTube videos, it is about growing. Plants are "the 101 metaphor for growing".
- **The message.** "I can't control the outcome, but I can control my effort, time and how consistent I am... maybe as
  the weeks go by we will find that the steps we decided to take each week are actually the outcome."
- It was a true story, from his own experience: mundane life can carry a story.

## 4. VISUAL vs ORAL Storytelling (Lofi Cinema)

- **The eyes-closed test.** "Cinema is a visual medium, but what if I told you that you could experience most
  mainstream movies today with your eyes closed?" Relying on spoken words makes films easy to follow without
  engaging with what is on screen, which turns viewers passive: "a medium for lazy readers". Even acclaimed,
  beautiful films lean on dialogue to move the plot.
- **Exposition by talk versus by sight.** Heist films usually plan first, in dialogue ("The elevator won't move
  without authorized fingerprint identification"), then execute. *Baby Driver* plays the planning scene so the
  exposition keeps a cinematic feel. A quoted director (Hitchcock [?]): "You cannot be satisfied just photographing a person speaking.
  That's only an extension of the theatre."
- **Why cinema is visual.** Early film had no sound, so filmmakers built a language of imagery and symbolism and used
  title cards sparingly, so audiences would not get bored. It let anyone in the world follow the story whatever their
  language. The tradition survived sound.
- **Great visual cinema invites you to take part.** In *No Country for Old Men* a killer avoids getting blood on his
  boots after a murder. Later, when another character's fate is open, the film cuts to him checking his boots: just
  enough to guide, not dictate.
- **Colour as the only clue.** *Better Call Saul*'s black-and-white teaser: Gene watches his old Saul Goodman ad, and
  the only colour in the shot is its reflection in his glasses.
- **Skip the planning.** *Better Call Saul*'s heists skip the planning and dive into execution, so we piece the plan
  together as it happens. "Audio gives us the text while the visuals reveal the subtext and true motivations."
- **Delayed motivation.** Long process scenes of characters doing something odd, for a reason we learn later. It
  rewards active viewing. Across a whole season, the long con on Howard shows the how first, and the episode "Plan and
  Execution" finally gives the why, paying off every clue. *The Killer* builds to an event that then does not happen.
- **Dialogue can still be visual.** In *The Social Network* Fincher turns a dialogue-heavy script into a visually
  engaging film.
- **Take-away.** Cinema at its best speaks through images and makes us active viewers. "Let your eyes do the
  listening."

## 5. The ONLY 5 Steps You Need to Tell Any Story (Visuals by Jussi)

"Beginning, middle and end" is too vague: without emotional stakes the audience will not care. The framework:

1. **Who is the main character?** Someone to follow and relate to.
2. **What do they want?**
3. **What stands in their way?** Want and obstacle open curiosity loops: viewers need to find out what happens.
4. **What do they do about it?**
5. **What is the outcome?** Action and outcome show cause and effect, which is satisfying. Then the **reaction** to
   the outcome makes viewers feel something.

His example: he is not someone who keeps things, but some things are worthless in money and mean the world. He
(character) wants to find his old CD player to hear his favourite band (want). He cannot find it (obstacle). He
searches somewhere else (action). He finds it (outcome), and the last shots show him enjoying the songs again
(reaction). "Skip any of these steps and your story feels flat, like a joke without a punchline." Everyday moments
(a CD player, a treadmill run, cooking) become cinematic with this structure.
