# Film craft: the rules a page borrows from cinema

Film has spent a hundred years telling stories without words. This file turns its working rules into rules for a
page: how to test whether the pictures are doing the work, the story spine every page and every stop needs, the
opening, the tension curve, where the eye goes, devices worth stealing, and the guardrails that keep clarity and
data intact. The shot list that puts these rules to work is in `storyboard.md`.

Sources: five videos Ash sent on 2026-10-19 (Pixar's *Up* montage, Philip Bloom's composition class, By Almén,
Lofi Cinema, Visuals by Jussi). Cleaned notes with every source line are in `background/story-plan-videos.md`.
For an abstract idea told as a world, read this with `living-metaphors.md`; it supplies the world, this file the
telling.

## Two tests: pictures covered, words covered

Run both on the storyboard, again on the screenshots, and give both to the story reviewer (`review.md`).

1. **Pictures covered (the eyes-closed test).** Read only the words. If the whole story still arrives, the page is
   "a radio play" with illustrations, and the pictures are decoration. Move a claim out of the copy and into the
   drawing: a relation, a change, a size, a feeling.
2. **Words covered (the mute test).** Look only at the frames. *Up*'s wedding with the sound off still shows a young
   couple and where each comes from. A reader should still see who this is about, what changes, and how it ends.

Both tests should partly fail, in the right places. **Words carry what pictures cannot:** names, numbers, quotes,
the ask, the next step. **Pictures carry what words would bloat:** how parts relate, what changes, what is bigger,
what it feels like. If a line of copy describes what the drawing already shows, cut the line.

## The story spine

"Beginning, middle and end" is too vague. Every page, every section and every stop of a journey answers five
questions, then shows a reaction. Skip one "and your story feels flat, like a joke without a punchline."

| Step | The question | On a proposal page | On a living-metaphor stop |
|---|---|---|---|
| Character | Who do we follow? | The reader's company, or one person, load or student in it | The vehicle and whoever rides it |
| Want | What do they want? | Their ask, in their words | What the light is looking for at this depth |
| Obstacle | What is in the way? | THE PROBLEM, specific | The dark, the current, the depth |
| Action | What do they do? | What we put in place (the solver chip) | The light reaches; the part acts |
| Outcome | What happens? | WHAT [CLIENT] GETS, in the marker | The part is found and named |
| Reaction | How does it feel? | The motif one stage further on, shown changed | The part stays lit as the journey moves on |

Want and obstacle open a question the reader wants closed. Action and outcome show cause and effect, which is
satisfying to watch. The reaction is the step drafts most often drop: the section ends on its outcome line and never
shows the world changed. End each section on the changed picture.

## The opening: context, a question, a feeling

The first screen still says who, what and what you get. On top of that it does three things:

- **Context:** what this is about, in one line.
- **A question the reader now needs answered.** "If there is nothing your audience wants to know, there is a high
  chance they skip." The question is about the how or the proof; the hero has already said the what.
- **A feeling.** The question should land somewhere the reader cares about: their worry, their pride, their deadline.

**Start at the strongest point, not the general one.** Almén could have opened on "YouTube is hard" and the video
would have seemed to be about YouTube. He opened on a friend asking "what would have had to happen in a week for you
to look back on it with pride?" Same story, different first scene. The start test: if the hero would open any
proposal, lesson or page in this field, start later in the story, at the moment only this reader has.

## Two layers: the journey and what it means

Almén's early stories had one layer, a character going from A to B. Voice-over adds the second: the inner journey.

- **The picture is the outer journey:** the truck's route, the dive, the climb, the motif building up.
- **The words are the inner journey:** what each step means for the reader. They are the voice-over, never a
  caption. "The truck reaches the depot" narrates the picture; "Nobody re-types the logbook again" is the meaning.
- **A metaphor lives in the second layer.** His dying houseplants said the video was about growing, not YouTube.
  Prefer metaphors everyone reads without a key, "the 101 metaphor": plants for growth, a cloud that becomes a child
  for a hope, a jar for savings. Check every fact a metaphor rests on (`living-metaphors.md`).

## Tension and rhythm

**Draw the tension curve before building.** Timeline along the bottom, tension up the side, one point per section.
Each ask raises tension (the problem), each answer releases it, the next ask raises it again; the close settles it.
Almén calls this the hook and release pattern, "one of the key things I learned about storytelling."
- No flat runs: three sections at the same tension read as a brochure.
- No two releases in a row without a new question between them.
- The deepest point (the hardest problem, the deepest layer) comes late, then a clear release into the close.

**Scroll length is importance.** Cut fast through what is not crucial: group small things, one quick beat, no
pinning. Hold long on what is: its own screen, one moving thing, room around it. Almén adds a few quick shots when
an edit drags and holds the long shot where the feeling matters.

**A drop is a beat.** After the peak, one near-empty screen (a single line, a held picture) lets the release land,
the way his sound cuts dead when the idea fails.

**When you cannot draw it, change the light.** He made anxiety with a low rumble. A page is silent, so carry a
feeling with the whole scene's light and colour: dusk, the night switch, one lit window in a dark frame.

## Where the eye goes

Composition guides the eye "to what we want it to see, or not see". Each frame has one first look; these decide it.

| Technique | In film | On the page |
|---|---|---|
| Brightness wins | "We are pulled towards brighter objects." A dark frame with one lit spot pulls you in | One lit thing in a dimmed scene: the veil, the searchlight, the lamps. Never two lit at once |
| Depth of field | Shallow focus marks who matters | Dim everything but the current part; past parts stay visible but dimmer, so the picture builds |
| Scale is importance | Filling the frame says this person matters; small in a wide says overwhelmed | Draw the part that matters biggest on its screen. A small figure in a wide scene shows a big task |
| Rule of thirds | Eyes on a crossing point | Put the focal point on a thirds crossing; the words sit in the space beside it |
| Central framing | For fast cuts, so the eye does not travel | One number or one line, dead centre, for a quick beat |
| Leading lines | Lines lead to the point of interest | A route, cable, river or road inside the drawing points at the next focal point, and at the end, at the ask |
| Lead space | A figure looking left sits on the right | Figures and vehicles face the way the story goes; the next words sit in the space they face. In a horizontal page the climber faces right |
| Frame within a frame | A doorway or window | A porthole, window or cutaway isolates one detail without a caption box |
| Shot sizes | Extreme wide to macro | Hero is the establishing shot (the whole world, small); sections are medium shots of one part; the proof is a close-up; the close pulls back to wide |
| Lens compression | A long lens pulls the background close | To say two distant things are linked, draw them in one frame, near each other |

Start wide, then go in. A page that opens on a close-up has no place; a page that stays wide never connects.

## Devices worth stealing

From *Up*'s married-life montage (a whole life in four and a half minutes, no words) and from *Better Call Saul*.

- **A thread that is also a measure.** The savings jar recurs through the montage and shows their progress; every
  setback (a flat tyre, a broken leg, a damaged roof) empties it. Give the page one recurring object whose state is
  the progress: the motif, the cable paid out, a jar of what the reader saves. Problems act on it, answers fill it.
- **Repetition with change shows time.** Carl's tie, knotted again and again, shows the years. Draw the same object
  several times, a little changed each time, instead of a timeline, when the point is "it kept happening" or "time
  passed".
- **Two frames for something hard.** A poster, then a doctor and body language: a child is not possible, said in two
  shots. Put a hard before-and-after in two pictures side by side, with no paragraph between them.
- **Character through contrast.** One side of the church loud, the other quiet. Show who the reader is, or today
  against with us, with small concrete details, not adjectives.
- **Setup and payoff.** The killer in *No Country for Old Men* checks his boots for blood; later, the film only shows
  him checking his boots. Plant one small detail early that the close pays off: a title block whose empty PILOT ROUTE
  cell shows from the first screen and is circled in marker at the close. Every plant pays off; every payoff was planted.
- **Show it working, skip the planning.** *Better Call Saul* skips the heist's planning and goes straight to the
  job, so we piece the plan together. Instead of a "how it works" section, show the offer working on the reader's own
  case, step by step (a worked example), then name the plan in a line.
- **Act, then words.** Delayed motivation in small: the part moves first, and its line writes after. "Audio gives the
  text; the visuals reveal the subtext." Use it inside a stop, never for the page's main point.
- **Full circle.** *Up* ends with Carl alone in the church where it began; the same place tells the audience the
  story is over. End on the hero's own scene and framing, changed: the motif finished, the world lit, the ask inside
  it.
- **Familiar things everyone reads.** A family, a new home, a cloud. Use the reader's own objects (their truck, their
  plant, their tablet) or universal ones; never a symbol that needs a key.

## Clarity guardrails: what not to borrow

Film can afford to be hard work. A reader of a page, a proposal or a lesson gives it thirty seconds. Keep these lines:

- **Ambiguity is for feeling and extras, never for facts.** "Guide, not dictate" is fine for a lamp's easter egg;
  the asks, numbers, names and the next step are always said plainly.
- **Never delay the main point.** The first screen says who, what and what you get. Delayed motivation works inside
  a stop or a section, after the reader already knows why they are there.
- **No twists.** *The Killer*'s promised event that never happens is a joke on the viewer. A proposal pays off
  everything it sets up.
- **Pictures show shape; the number is printed beside it.** Every figure comes from the host's facts sheet, written as a figure.
  A drawing's sizes match the numbers; no lens-style distortion in anything that carries data.
- **Silent by default.** Sound plays only after a click and Ash has not asked for it. Light and colour carry mood.
- **The reader is never asked to decode.** If the mute test needs a key to pass, the metaphor is wrong.
