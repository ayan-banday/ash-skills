# Story: the argument, the page order, the section anatomy

A proposal site wins when a reader who skims headings, drawings and highlighted phrases can say back: "they heard
what we asked, they showed how each ask gets answered, and they told me the one thing to do next." This file covers
the argument you write first, the default page order, how each section is built, the close, and the other
routes the same brief could take.

## Write the argument before any markup

Fill this in plain text and show it to Ash in the story-map playback. It decides the page order.

1. **Reader:** who opens the link, what they already know, what they distrust.
2. **Their asks, in their words:** quote each one verbatim from the meeting, email or brief. Never paraphrase inside quote marks.
3. **One belief after the first screen:** the sentence a reader should hold after three seconds.
4. **Per ask:** the claim (how we answer it), the proof (a fact from facts.md or a worked example), the visual (a catalogue ID).
5. **The one action:** what to choose, who to contact, what arrives next and by when.
6. **8 to 12 things only this client has:** names, places, materials, rituals, artefacts, their own phrases
   (TVS: "the TVS Way", Dojo, Hosur, the kolam at a Tamil threshold). Build the motif and the copy from these.
   **Competitor test:** if a rival of the client fits the same hero sentence, the idea is generic. Rewrite it.

## The default page order (the TVS order)

Built from Ash's own clarity model: open with the reader's asks in their words, then show where each ask is answered.

1. **Hero, one full screen** (100svh, nothing from the next section visible). The point in one line, the motif
   drawing itself on. "What TVS asked for, and how JAIN delivers it." The lede names the sender, what they would
   actually put in place, and the first step ("Tidewater Studio fits sensors, a driver tablet and a trip record to
   your trucks... then pick one route for a pilot"). Both reviewers failed heroes that said only the page's shape.
2. **What you told us:** one card per ask. The need in their words, an action headline, coloured "Answered by" chips.
3. **How to read the motif** (only when the motif maps to the asks): which part stands for which need.
4. **One section per ask, each built the same way:** badge with that part lit, a "You told us" block worded exactly
   like the card, one example visual, the team or capability visual (snake, switchboard, river).
5. **Plain-words translator** when the offer has jargon the reader needs (we call it, it means, so at [client]).
6. **The thread section** when the client named a value of their own ("The Indian way of doing things").
7. **How it all connects:** one map of every ask to every team (constellation, transit map, switchboard).
8. **One or two worked examples, labelled as examples.** "An example, chosen to show how we would work."
9. **How we start:** three pictured steps and one named contact.

Drop a step when the brief has nothing for it. Never add a section with no claim in it.

Then tell it like film: each section runs the story spine (character, want, obstacle, action, outcome, reaction),
the sections together make a tension curve, and the close returns to the hero's scene, changed. Write the
storyboard with the story-plan skill before any markup.

## Section anatomy: one need, one section

Every need section follows the same order, so the reader learns the rhythm once:

- **Badge:** the motif with this need's part lit (assets/motifs: `badge()`).
- **Label + headline:** label says which ask ("ASK 2 · THE CAB"); the headline is the action ("Put the logbook on a screen").
- **You told us:** their words, verbatim, in the quote block (PPL-16), only when the "What you told us" section is
  several screens back; when it sits just above, name the ask by its part and skip the repeat.
- **Problem first:** THE PROBLEM → the solver chip → what they do (small, muted) → WHAT [CLIENT] GETS (marker highlight).
- **One visual** picked by content shape from catalogue/00-picker.md. A section gets one drawn thing, not three.
- **Chips:** the same team chip colours everywhere that team appears.

## The motif carries the story

The motif is a drawing from the client's world whose structure maps to the proposal: centre = the client's own
way, strands or parts = the asks, repeated parts = the teams or steps. It draws itself on in the hero, appears as a
lit badge in each section, and resolves at the close. If the motif cannot map to the asks, use it only in the hero
and the badges; never invent a mapping the reader has to decode. Match the reference photo or object exactly:
Ash rejected the first kolams because they read as "circles" and "chain-link fence".

## The close: three pictured steps and one person

The proven close (PPL-28 with PPL-27 and PPL-01). Each step is a small drawing plus one line:
1. **Choose:** the real options with our recommendation hand-circled.
2. **Talk to one person:** their name and role. One point of contact, never a team inbox.
3. **Get this next:** the exact deliverable and a relative time ("a one-page plan within a week").
Then the contact card: drawn portrait in the page's own style, name, one line on what to send and what comes
back, the email as selectable text, and a Copy button (mailto is unreliable in artifacts).
Never a dated plan unless Ash asks for one. Never "Let's talk" or "Get in touch".

**The motif's close.** When the page has a drawn motif, the close shows it finished and resolves every ask on it:
in the Coastline example the truck returns in paper line on the dark band with its sensors, the driver tablet and the
route as the trip record, and the title block gains an empty PILOT ROUTE cell circled in marker. Then the contact card.
Say the ask once: if the drawing and the contact card already carry it, drop the numbered steps.

## Asks, said once and named by their part

Quote the reader's asks once, in the "What you told us" section. Later sections name the ask by its part of the
motif ("THE BOX", "THE CAB", "THE TRIP") instead of re-quoting it or numbering it; when the drawing orders the parts
left to right, numbers fight the drawing (the reviewer met "Ask 2" before "Ask 1"). Put the "who does what" section
after the asks, just before the close, so the asks run unbroken and the one contact line sits next to her card.

## Narrative frames (when the default order does not fit)

- **You asked, here is where it is answered** (default): asks first, one section each.
- **Two roads:** today versus with us, for a decision between acting and waiting (CHG-37).
- **Follow one thing:** one load, one patient, one student carried through the whole page on a pinned stage (ARC-02).
- **Build-up:** the finished deliverable assembles one part per section and the close shows it whole (ARC-03).
- **Mirror halves:** the client's day today, then the same day with us (ARC-04).
- **SCQA:** situation, complication, question, answer, for a board that wants the logic first.
Pick one and say which in the story map.

## Routes: other forms the same brief can take

Offer these when the brief fits them better, and record which one you chose:
- **A PDF brochure** via polished-doc, when the reader will print it or forward an attachment.
- **A static explainer page** via brief-page-udyaan, when the reader will actually read and wants no motion.
- **This skill's scroll site**, when the reader won't read and the link is the deliverable.
Alternatives Ash weighed and rejected for TVS: a claude.com-style timeline rail with a role picker (not
sequential), a transit-map metaphor in place of the mind map (kept as a fallback), a static glossary in place of
the translator, a page-long scroll thread (distracting), a particle-swarm hero, a paper leave-behind with a QR
code, horizontal step strips ("far too lazy"). The full list is in catalogue/considered-and-cut.md.
