Clear Documents: a skill that makes any document easier to read

The skill takes a document you already have, keeps its structure, and makes it clearer: plainer words, a calmer layout, a harmonious palette, and pictures where they explain faster than text. This PDF was built with those same rules, so every page doubles as an example.

How to give feedback. Every part and visual has a number. Reply with the number and the change you want, for example "5.10: the funnel labels are too small" or "9.2: keep hand-lettered labels".

Part 1 · What the skill does

The skill starts from the document you already have

You hand it a document: a draft report, a course page, a proposal, meeting notes. It hands back the same document, with the same sections in the same order, written plainer and laid out so a busy reader gets it on the first pass.

What stays and what changes

Stays
  • Section order and what each section covers
  • Every fact and number you gave
  • Your voice, including the strange, vivid lines
  • Roughly the same length
Changes
  • Heading wording, so each heading states its point
  • Hedges, filler, and repeats, cut
  • Layout, spacing, and colour, set by Parts 6 and 7
  • Long lists, grouped, nested, or mapped as in Part 4
  • Visuals, added where they explain faster than words

When the skill wants to add something the writer never said, such as a deadline or a reason, it marks the addition and asks. Part 8 shows one.

Part 2 · Writing

Ten writing rules in three groups, each shown on a real sentence

Every rule came from an edit to a real draft. The guardrail is meaning and feeling: if an edit makes a line plainer but the writer would no longer say it, the edit is wrong.

Group A

Cut what carries no weight

Hedges, untrue framings, setup lines, and decoration.

1

Cut hedges the claim does not need

Before"For you, it's probably the project."After"For you, it's the project."

Keep a hedge when cutting it makes the line false: "Sometimes, the most useful step looks like a step in a different direction."

2

Say what is true instead of what is untrue

Before"Changing direction isn't always about doing something different. It's to do what you love for a different reason."After"Sometimes changing direction means doing what you already love, for a different reason."

One deliberate use at an emotional peak is allowed. As a habit, it makes the reader hold a wrong idea first.

3

Cut setup lines and repeats

Before"The thing I want you to take away from that is that I have excellent lungs."After"What I want you to take away is that I have excellent lungs."

Also cut "Here's the thing", "It's worth noting", and a second sentence that says the first one again.

4

Cut detail that serves no point, and keep detail that pays off later

BeforeA paragraph deriving loop-the-loop height from energy conservation.AfterCut. "I didn't know what torque was" stays, because the ending calls back to it.

Test: delete it. If nothing later stops working, it was decoration.

Group B

Make it specific

Named things, one running example, and a visible result.

5

Name the specific thing

Before"whenever I got curious about anything that wasn't on the syllabus"After"when I asked my teachers about quantum field theory"

A category asks the reader to imagine. A named thing lets them see it.

6

Carry one example all the way through

BeforeFour examples across three steps: masters abroad, 10 countries, no experience, product-market fit.AfterOne example, "a job in a field you're not in yet", followed through every step.
7

Show what changes for the reader

Before"I bought a course to learn how to open a conversation with a stranger." The paragraph ends there.After"…with a stranger. That's how I built my first business."

Impact can be a result, a feeling, or a next step. It needs no statistic.

Group C

Connect it and keep the voice

Sections that hand off, headings that answer, and lines that still sound like the writer.

8

End a section on the question the next one answers

Before"Arnold Schwarzenegger is a good example."After"But I didn't know what to do today. The answer came from the last person I expected: Arnold Schwarzenegger."
9

Make every heading give the answer

Before"Remaining issues"After"Point 4 is the one the reviewer will raise again"

Test: read only the headings. The reader should get the whole argument.

10

Keep the writer's voice

Keep"That thought made my bones wanna leave my body."AlsoMix long and short sentences, use contractions, and swap em dashes for commas or colons.
Part 3 · When to draw

A visual earns its place when the content has a shape

The test: would a reader get this faster from a picture than from the sentence? If yes, draw it.

If two sentences already do the job, keep the sentences. Clarity decides, never decoration.

Pick a visual by answering two questions

Keep the words when

  • A short story already works. In the lead-reply demo, the customer's story stayed as two sentences.
  • There is one number. Bold it inside the sentence.
  • The picture only decorates. Every shape must stand for something the text says.
  • The reader needs to look things up. Use a table instead.

Sketches and charts can share a page. Stick figures show people and situations, and clean charts show amounts and order. A page can hold one of each when both help.

Part 4 · Long lists

Long lists get grouped, nested, or mapped

A flat list of twelve items asks the reader to hold twelve things. Three groups of four ask them to hold three. The skill picks a structure by how long the list is and whether its items connect.

4.4 · Mapped: the same skill on one page

A mind map trades detail for the whole picture. Use it when the reader needs to see how the parts relate, and keep the nested version nearby for detail.

4.5 · Three more shapes for a long list

  • Decision tree, when the reader must pick one item. Part 3 turns twelve visuals into two questions.
  • Tiered table, when items rank by importance. Part 7 splits ten design rules into three tiers.
  • Matrix, when items differ on two traits at once. See 5.12.
Part 5 · The visual catalogue

Twelve visuals in three groups, and when each one helps

Each entry shows when to use the visual, when to skip it, and a working example. The numbers inside the examples are illustrative.

Group A

People and situations

Stick figures show who is involved and what happens to them.

5.1 · Stick-figure scene: show who is involved and what pulls at them

Use whenThe reader needs to picture a person and the forces on them.Skip whenA name and a role say enough.

5.2 · Story strip: show a mistake unfolding, step by step

Use whenEach step leads to the next and the last one costs something.Skip whenThe steps carry no cost or surprise.

5.3 · Contrast pair: two cases that look alike, with one deciding difference

Use whenOne detail decides the outcome and the reader keeps missing it.Skip whenThe cases differ in many ways at once.

5.4 · Hub: one thing connected to many parts

Use whenThe reader needs to see everyone or everything one item touches.Skip whenThe parts connect to each other in a chain. Use a flow.
Group B

Steps, time, and action

Shapes that show order, duration, and the one thing to do.

5.5 · Flow: steps in order, with the slow step marked

Use whenOrder matters and one step causes the trouble.Skip whenThere are two steps, or every step is fine.

5.6 · Timeline: when things happen, and where time is lost

Use whenDates or durations carry the point.Skip whenOnly the order matters. Use a flow.

5.7 · Stairs: levels that build on each other

Use whenEach level needs the one below it.Skip whenThe items can happen in any order.

5.8 · Decision box: the one action, at the end

Use whenThe reader must decide or act, once, by a date.Skip whenNothing is asked of the reader.

Approve the rota by Friday 23 October

If you approve, Sara sets up the form routing on Monday 26 October and lead duty starts that afternoon. If you want a different rota, reply with the days you can't cover.

Group C

Amounts and choices

Clean charts for shares, drops, and trade-offs.

5.9 · Dot split: a share the reader can count

Use whenThe group is small enough to draw one dot per person.Skip whenThe total runs into the thousands. Use bars.

5.10 · Funnel: a group that narrows stage by stage

Use whenPeople drop out at each stage and the drop is the story.Skip whenThe stages do not shrink.

5.11 · Bars: two or three amounts side by side

Use whenThe difference between amounts is the point.Skip whenThere is one amount. Bold it in the sentence.

5.12 · Matrix: a choice decided by two factors

Use whenItems differ on effort and impact at the same time.Skip whenOne factor decides. Use a ranked list.
Part 6 · Colour

Each document gets its own harmonious palette

A small script picks a random base hue and a harmony type, then builds eleven colour roles inside muted, professional limits. Every text colour is checked for contrast before the palette is used, and the seed is recorded so the same palette can be rebuilt.

Five harmony types, each a rule on the colour wheel

Analogous and split-complementary are the quiet defaults. Complementary and triadic appear less often, because they only stay professional when the accent is kept small.

The palette covers 60-30-10 of the page

Eleven roles, each with a job

RoleJobLimit
InkBody text, tinted toward the primary hue7:1 contrast on the page
MutedCaptions, labels, footers4.5:1 contrast
LineRules and table bordersLight enough to stay quiet
Primary and primary softHeadings, key shapes, and their tinted fills4.5:1, muted chroma
Accent and accent softOne or two highlights per page3:1 for shapes
Alert and okCost and success, always with a word as wellKept apart in hue and lightness
Background and surfacePage and panels, near whiteWhite for print

Six palettes from the script

Strip order: background, ink, muted, line, primary, primary soft, accent, accent soft, alert, ok.

What the palette never does

  • No neon and no gradients. Flat, muted fills print cleanly and read calmly.
  • No colour as the only signal. Red and green always come with a word, an icon, or a position.
  • No equal-strength opposites. In a complementary palette, one colour leads and the other stays small.
  • No grey text on a tinted fill without a contrast check.
Part 7 · Design basics

Design basics decide what the reader sees first

Twelve principles from Figma's design library, translated from screens to pages. Six of them do most of the work on a document, and the page below marks where each one shows.

Ten rules the skill enforces, in three tiers

Before delivery, the skill runs four checks on every page:

  • Squint: are the top three things obvious?
  • Greyscale: does everything still read?
  • Left edge: does everything line up?
  • Orphans: is any heading or caption cut off from its block?
Part 8 · Before and after

A memo made clearer without changing its shape

The memo keeps its subject line and both sections. The words, the headings, and the ask change. Struck words were cut, and highlighted words are new.

Before
Subject: Update on the onboarding situation
Overview

Hi all, just wanted to give a quick update on where things are with onboarding. It's not really about the tools, it's more about the process, I think. New hires are kind of taking a while to get set up, probably around 9 days on average, which is something that we should maybe look into at some point.

Next steps

We could potentially look at having a checklist, or perhaps a buddy system, or maybe both. Let me know your thoughts.

After
Subject: New hires take 9 days to set up
The delay comes from our process

New hires take about 9 days to get set up. The cause is the process, so new tools would leave it unchanged.

Pick a checklist, a buddy, or both

Two fixes are on the table: a setup checklist, and a buddy for each new hire. Reply by Friday with the one you prefer, or both.

What the skill did, and why

  1. Kept the shape. Same subject line, same two sections, same facts.
  2. Rewrote both headings to give the answer. "Overview" and "Next steps" named slots. The new ones state the point.
  3. Cut seven hedges: kind of, probably, maybe, twice, at some point, potentially, perhaps.
  4. Turned the negative parallelism into a plain cause: the delay comes from the process.
  5. Flagged one addition for the writer. "Reply by Friday" is new. The skill marks it and asks before keeping it.
  6. Added no visual. The memo is two short sections, so words do the job.
Part 9 · Your call

Six decisions I need from you

Each answer changes how the skill gets built, so these come before anything else.

9.1

One skill or two?

I suggest two. Clear Documents handles writing, structure, visuals, and export. Document Palette handles colour harmony and design basics, so brief-page-udyaan and future skills can use it too.

9.2

Hand-lettered labels in stick-figure sketches?

Part 5 uses a handwritten font inside sketches and a clean font in charts. The mix signals "sketch" and "data" at a glance. The other option is clean labels everywhere for formal documents.

9.3

A new palette for every document, or one per project?

Random per document keeps things fresh. One seed per project keeps a series, such as a five-day course, looking like one set.

9.4

PDF as the default output?

The skill writes once and exports to PDF, Word, Google Docs, or HTML. I set PDF as the default because you asked for one here.

9.5

Did I read "approve the rota by Friday the 23rd" right?

I took it as liking the closing decision box from the demo, so it became visual 5.8. If you meant something else, tell me what.

9.6

Are the list sizes right?

Part 4 keeps lists of up to 7 items, groups 8 to 15 into three, nests 16 to 30, and maps anything bigger or linked. Tell me if a different cut-off fits how you read.

Send your feedback by section number

Reply with the numbers you want changed and your answers to 9.1 to 9.6. I'll fold them into the skill and send it back for approval.
