## The test and the guardrail

**The test.** Keep a sentence only if it adds a fact, a picture, a feeling, or an action, and a reader gets it on the first pass.

**The guardrail.** The meaning and the feeling stay the writer's. If an edit makes a line plainer but the writer would no longer say it, the edit is wrong. A clearer document that no longer sounds like its author has failed.

Why these rules exist: busy readers give each sentence a second or two. Filler costs that time and pays nothing back, and hedges make the writer sound unsure of things they are sure of.

## Group A: Cut what carries no weight

### 1. Cut hedges the claim does not need

| Bad | Good |
|---|---|
| "I approached life **a little** like that balloon." | "I approached life like that balloon." |
| "For you, it's **probably** the project." | "For you, it's the project." |
| "For you, **it might be**: 'In two years…'" | "For you: 'In two years…'" |
| "Real estate **helped give** him that freedom." | "Real estate gave him that freedom." |
| "New hires are **kind of** taking a while, **probably** around 9 days, which we should **maybe** look into **at some point**." | "New hires take about 9 days to get set up." |

**Keep the hedge when:**
- **Cutting it makes the line false.** "Sometimes, the most useful step looks like a step in a different direction." Without "sometimes" it is untrue.
- **It is honesty the next line depends on.** "I might get the answers wrong. So I try things…"
- **It is an estimate.** "About 40 minutes a day" is accurate, not weak.

### 2. Say what is true instead of what is untrue

"It isn't X, it's Y" makes the reader hold a wrong idea first, then throw it away.

| Bad | Good |
|---|---|
| "Changing direction isn't always about doing something different. It's to do what you love for a different reason." | "Sometimes changing direction means doing what you already love, for a different reason." |
| "I didn't need a bigger goal. I needed to find what was in my way." | "I needed to find what was in my way, the way Arnold did." |
| "It's not really about the tools, it's more about the process." | "The cause is the process, so new tools would leave it unchanged." |
| "Speed does not choose your future. It only gets you there sooner." | "Speed decides how soon you get there. You decide where." |
| "They are patterns in how founders think, not signs of carelessness." | "They are patterns in how founders think." |

**One exception:** a single deliberate use at the emotional peak of a long piece, the way a strong speaker might say "Silence does not protect us. It isolates us." Once, on purpose. If you keep one, say so in the hand-off note.

### 3. Cut setup lines and repeats

A setup line announces the next sentence instead of being it.

| Bad | Good |
|---|---|
| "The thing I want you to take away from that is that I have excellent lungs." | "What I want you to take away is that I have excellent lungs." |
| "Look at every skill and ask two things: which ones…" | "Look at every skill and ask which ones…" |
| "But what I didn't know was what I should be doing today." | "But I didn't know what to do today." |
| "That's what I did with engineering. The skill I loved most…" | "With engineering, the skill I loved most…" |
| "Just wanted to give a quick update on where things are with onboarding." | Delete. The subject line already says it. |
| "For Arnold, they served a purpose he could explain. Each gave him something he needed." | "For Arnold, each one gave him something he needed." |

Also cut: "Here's the thing", "Here's what it looks like", "It's worth noting", "Let's dive in", "In this document we will", "As mentioned above", and a sentence that labels a pattern the reader just saw ("Both groups make the same error").

### 4. Cut detail that serves no point, and keep detail that pays off later

**Test:** delete it. If nothing later stops working, it was decoration.

| Bad | Good |
|---|---|
| A paragraph deriving loop-the-loop height from energy conservation, in a talk about losing the joy of building. | Cut. It shows off and argues against the writer's own point. |
| Cutting "I didn't know what torque was" because it sounds technical. | Keep. The ending calls back to it: "He had no idea what torque was. He knew exactly what he wanted to make." |

## Group B: Make it specific

### 5. Name the specific thing

A category asks the reader to imagine. A named thing lets them see it.

| Bad | Good |
|---|---|
| "whenever I got curious about anything that wasn't on the syllabus" | "when I asked my teachers about quantum field theory" |
| "research the idea" | "search for startup ideas on YouTube and Instagram" |
| "a small fee" | "Rs 3 per kg, taken out of what the market pays them" |
| "stakeholders" | "finance, legal, and the client" |

### 6. Carry one example all the way through

Four examples across three steps make the reader restart four times. One example followed through every step lets them watch the idea work.

| Bad | Good |
|---|---|
| Direction: "move abroad for a master's" or "expand to 10 countries". Barrier: "no work experience" or "no product-market fit". | One example for the whole room, "a job in a field you're not in yet", followed through Direction, Barrier, and Decomposition. |

Also check that the example obeys the rule it teaches. A list defined as "trainable abilities" cannot use "No idea how to get an internship" as an item; that is a problem. "Writing a CV that gets a callback" is an ability.

### 7. Show what changes for the reader

After a claim, say what it changes. Impact can be a result, a feeling, or a next step. **It needs no statistic**, and the skill never invents one.

| Bad | Good |
|---|---|
| "I bought a course to learn how to open a conversation with a stranger." The paragraph ends there. | "…with a stranger. That's how I built my first business." |
| "The biggest problem is spoilage. It's worth thinking about why." | "The biggest problem is spoilage. A company can't open two new cities if a fifth of its fruit rots." |

If the writer has a real number, use it. If they don't, describe the change in words. Never make a number up.

## Group C: Connect it and keep the voice

### 8. End a section on the question the next one answers

A reader should feel pulled into the next section, not dropped into it.

| Bad | Good |
|---|---|
| End of one section: "…what I should be doing today." Start of the next: "Arnold Schwarzenegger is a good example." | "But I didn't know what to do today to make that future possible. The answer came from the last person I expected: Arnold Schwarzenegger." |
| "I had to break it down like Arnold." (Arnold never broke anything down.) | "Arnold knew where he was going. He saw what would stop him, and worked on that first." Then: "So I turned what Arnold did into three questions." |

Before writing a new bridge, look for it in the writer's own lines. "A company is a machine too. Its parts are people" was built from three phrases already in the draft.

### 9. Make every heading give the answer

See the Headings section below. Test: read only the headings. The reader should get the whole argument.

### 10. Keep the writer's voice

- **Keep their strange, vivid lines:** "made my bones wanna leave my body", "full cartoon hypnosis, spirals in both eyes". Clarity edits remove filler, never personality.
- **Mix long and short sentences.** Matched lengths read as machine-written.
- **Use contractions** and the words the writer would say out loud.
- **No em dashes.** Use a comma, a colon, or a full stop.
- **No triplets added for rhythm** ("It was X. It was Y. And it was Z." becomes one list). Real lists stay: "chapters completed, questions solved, marks earned".
- **Fragments the writer chose stay** in speeches and personal pieces. In reports and proposals, write full sentences.

## What you may add, and what you must flag

The skill changes how the document reads, never what it claims.

- **Free to change:** wording, heading wording, order of sentences inside a paragraph, list structure, layout, visuals that show what the text already says.
- **Flag and ask:** any new fact, reason, deadline, number, name, or promise. Mark it in the document as a highlighted insertion and list it in the hand-off note. Example: the memo's new "Reply by Friday" was marked and listed.
- **Never:** invent a statistic, a quote, a result, or a source. Never change a number the writer gave.

## Headings

A heading states the point of its section. A reader scanning headings alone gets the argument.

| Bad (names a slot) | Good (gives the answer) |
|---|---|
| Overview | New hires take 9 days to set up |
| Next steps | Pick a checklist, a buddy, or both |
| The problem | Leads wait for the morning sort before anyone owns them |
| Remaining issues | Point 4 is the one the reviewer will raise again |
| Feedback point 2 | The bridge is already in your draft |

**Clever headings fail.** A heading passes when someone who hasn't read the section knows exactly what it covers. It is one of three things: a plain question the reader would ask, a concrete answer in everyday words, or a plain label for a recap or reference part ("Key takeaways", "Sources").

- Bad: "Two pictures to think with, and three rules that hold everywhere". It counts the contents and hides them.
- Bad: "Three rules, two pictures and two questions to carry into your own tests"
- Bad: "Symmetry is the language of trust". An aphorism makes the reader decode it.
- Good: "How do you know people will pay for your SaaS before you build it?" A question the reader would ask.
- Good: "New hires take 9 days to set up". A concrete answer.
- Good: "Key takeaways". A plain label for a recap.
- **Why:** a clever heading is a puzzle the reader solves before reading. A plain one tells them where they are.

**Test:** read the heading alone. If you can't say what the section will tell you, rewrite it in plainer words.

- At most three visible heading levels.
- A plain question works as a heading in a friendly page ("Who is this for?").
- No heading claims how many people do something ("Most founders get this wrong").
- Keep the writer's section order. Rewording a heading is fine; merging or reordering sections needs the writer's yes.

## Emphasis

- One or two bold phrases per paragraph, never a whole sentence. Reading only the bold should give the point.
- No stacked styles (bold plus colour plus underline on the same words).
- One callout per page at most.
- Colour is never the only signal. Red and green always come with a word.

## Banned phrases and the scan

Run `python scripts/scan_copy.py doc.html`. It flags em dashes, lead-ins, hedges, negative parallelism, generic headings, frequency words in headings, paragraphs over three sentences, and parenthetical asides.

Hits inside quoted "before" examples are expected. Every other hit gets fixed or consciously kept (and kept hits go in the hand-off note).

Words to avoid: demystify, leverage, journey, empower, crucial, optimize, unlock, elevate, seamless, game-changer, landscape, robust, holistic, synergy, delve.
