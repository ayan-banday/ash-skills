# Writing rules

These rules cover the words only. Page order is in `page-shape.md`, drawings in `visual-patterns.md`. The rules exist because the reader is alone with the page: nobody is there to explain a term, answer "what does this mean for me?", or fill a gap. They give each sentence about two seconds. A sentence that does not add a fact, an example, or an action costs that time and gives nothing back.

## The one test

Keep a sentence only if both answers are yes:

1. Would a 7th grader understand it on the first read?
2. Does it add a fact, an example, or an action?

If either is no, rewrite it or delete it.

## Voice

Plain, warm, spoken. Like a friendly senior explaining something in a corridor.

- Use contractions (it's, doesn't, they'll).
- Use the plain word: "figure out", "spot", "find", "check", "write down".
- No curses, no war metaphors, no hype punctuation, no praise of the reader.
- Warmth comes from naming the reader's real situation, not from compliments.
- A brief speaks to its reader: "you" is the teammate or boss reading it, and Ash is named ("Ash wants to..."), so the page still reads right when it is forwarded.
- When the page describes a program for many people (students, customers), write about them in third person ("a student meets the team") in the same plain tone.

## Sentence rules

**1. Say it once, plainly.**
Bad: A few months later, one of two things has happened. They never started, or they built something nobody uses.
Good: A few months later they either never started or built something nobody uses.

**2. Every sentence adds a fact, an example, or an action.** Cut sentences that comment on the text, label a pattern, or reassure.
Bad: All three are real. Both groups make the same error.
Good: (delete both)

**3. Put the impact in the claim's own sentence.**
Bad: Deadlines are one of the problems. It's worth thinking about why.
Good: Deadlines come from nobody, so work stalls even when we stay in touch.

**4. Show what the reader (or the person in the plan) does.**
Bad: Students need a sense of belonging in a supportive community.
Good: Each student joins a team of four from their own campus, so three people notice when an update is missing.

**5. Specific beats general.** Name the tool, the place, the number, the day.
Bad: Students do some research on the problem.
Good: The Researcher talks to five students who have the problem and writes down the exact words they use.

**6. No lead-ins.** Banned: "Here's the thing", "Here's what it looks like", "Let's dive in", "It's worth noting", "Now you try it".
Bad: Here's what a student does on Day 1:
Good: On Day 1 a student meets the team and hears the Day 14 date.

**7. State the point positively (no "not X, but Y").**
Bad: This isn't a workshop, it's a project.
Good: Students spend 14 days building one project.
Bad: They are patterns in how teams work, not signs of carelessness.
Good: They are patterns in how teams work.

**8. Write full thoughts.** No fragments and no one-word sentences.
Bad: No partner needed. No budget needed. Just a team.
Good: A team can start without a partner or a budget.

**9. Mix sentence lengths.** Three sentences of the same shape in a row read as machine-written.
Bad: Students learn the method. Students build the project. Students present the result.
Good: Students learn the method by building one project, then present it to judges and an audience on Day 14.

**10. No em dashes (or en dashes).** Use a comma, a period, or "to" in ranges ("Days 2 to 4").
Bad: The team builds, and the dashboard shows it, all in one place.
Good: The team builds, and the dashboard shows it.

**11. Define a term where it first appears, in plain words.**
Bad: Teams pass Gate 1 on Day 4.
Good: A gate is a short checklist at a set point. Teams pass Gate 1 on Day 4 when the project doc has dated milestones.

**12. No hedges and no hype.** Remove "might", "perhaps", "somewhat", "arguably", "tends to", "kind of", "sort of", "in some ways". If something is uncertain, say exactly what is uncertain.
Bad: This could potentially help improve retention.
Good: We set retention targets after the first cohort, so they rest on real numbers.

**13. No invented statistics or frequency claims.** Use only numbers you were given. If you were given none, use none. Design choices you propose (a 3-minute demo, two updates a week) are fine as plan details because they are the plan, and you list them in your hand-off note so the user can change them.
Bad: Most students drop off in week 2.
Good: Students drop off quietly, and nobody notices.

**14. Count what you list.** If a heading says "five gates", the list has five items.

**15. Numbers are exact.** "20 minutes" beats "a while". "Under six partners" is exact enough when that is what you know.

**16. Questions in the body.** Headings are questions; body text answers them. Don't end paragraphs with rhetorical questions.

**17. Short paragraphs.** One to three sentences. One idea per paragraph.

## The page explains itself

Nobody presents a brief page. Write every line so it works with no one in the room.

**1. Explain a term where it first appears, in plain words, then name it.**
Bad: "Each plugin gets its own namespace."
Good: "Inside Claude Code, a skill from a plugin is typed with the plugin's name in front, like `/visual:brief-page`. That prefix is the plugin's **namespace**."

**2. Never point at something the reader can't see.** No "as discussed", "the usual setup", "this" or "it" without the thing named nearby.
Bad: "This fixes it."
Good: "One master copy fixes the drift: a change made once reaches every project."

**3. Introduce each thing at the moment it's needed,** with what it is and why it exists. A file, tool or name never appears before the page has said what it is.

**4. Say what it means for the reader.** Every section has at least one line on what changes for them, what they'd do, or what they'd notice.
Bad: "Superpowers will be removed."
Good: "Superpowers stops loading in every session, so **you'll no longer see Claude stop to brainstorm** before small jobs."

**5. Name the open questions as open.** A reader alone can't ask whether something is decided. Mark it: "Still open: whether the plan plugin starts empty."

## Headings

**Every section heading is the question the reader would ask at that point, naming the thing and the situation.** The section answers it, so that after reading, the reader could explain the answer in two or three sentences. Someone who reads only the headings sees the questions the page settles; someone who reads only the first line under each heading has the answers.

**Each question names its thing,** so it could only sit on this page.
- Bad: "What goes where?" / "Why rename them?" / "What changes?"
- Good: "Which of Ash's skills go into which plugin?" / "Why does each skill get a new name that says what it does?" / "What changes for Mohit when Superpowers is removed?"
- **Why:** "what goes where" fits a hundred pages. Naming the thing tells the reader exactly what the section covers.

**Each question names the situation,** so it makes sense read alone.
- Bad: "How do you split up a big system when you can't see the end?"
- Good: "How do you split a big AI system like Charlotte into pieces when you can't see the full picture of what you're building?"
- **Why:** "the end" could mean anything. The situation tells the reader which problem the section solves.

**Never a summary headline or a slogan.**
- Bad: "One master copy, everywhere" / "Less is more" / "Symmetry is the language of trust"
- Bad: "Five plugins and three rules to keep them tidy" (counts the contents, hides them)
- Bad: "Under six partners can't give 80 students real work" (an answer headline; it was the old house style. The answer now goes in the first line under the question.)
- **Why:** a slogan is a puzzle the reader decodes before reading. A question tells them where they are and what they'll know after.

**Plain labels are allowed for page furniture only:** "The short answer", "You'll decide", "Still open", "Key takeaways", "Sources". Nothing else gets a label in place of a question.

**Ask the question the reader would ask, in their words.** "What does a plugin actually change for me?" beats "What are the operational implications of plugins?" If the reader is Mohit, it is Mohit's question.

**Test:** read the heading alone, out of context. If you can't say what the section will tell you and which situation it's about, rewrite it.

Also:
- Never put a frequency claim in a heading ("Most teams fail at X").
- Never name the scene ("Mohit opens Claude Code"). Ask the question the scene answers.
- One line where possible; the thing's name early.
- The small label above a heading (the eyebrow) may number the section ("3 of 7") because it is navigation. The heading itself is the question.

## Emphasis

- Bold one or two **phrases** per paragraph. Never a whole sentence.
- Reading only the bold should give the point.
Bad: **You should write down your problem and then think about who else has it.**
Good: Write down your problem, then write **who else has it**.

## Lists

- Three or more parallel items can be a list. Up to 7 items stay a list; past 7, group them under two or three named groups (polished-doc' `references/lists.md` has the full regrouping table).
- **Before keeping a list, check its shape.** If the items happen in order, split a whole, sort into groups, change from before to after, or compete as options, draw that shape instead (`visual-patterns.md`). A list stays a list when its items have no relation to show.
- Each item opens with a bold phrase that names a concrete action or thing: **A question:** what the student wants to find out.
- Say it, then show it: every claim is followed by a concrete example.
- When teaching a rule, show a good pick and a bad pick, each leading with the pick and then its impact.

## Examples (the named person or project that carries the plan)

- Make the first example the simplest possible: three short lines (goal, problem, solution).
- One idea per example.
- The example must actually follow the rule it teaches. If it would fail the rule, replace it.
- Invented details (names, quotes, items on a board) are fine. Do not label them "(made up)" inside sentences. Put one line in the footer: "Meera and her team show how the plan works in practice." and list the invented items in your hand-off note.
- Never reuse an example in the same document.
- The example should build something visible: a project panel that fills in, a doc, a dashboard row. Seeing the artifact is more convincing than a description.

## Writing about people and programs

- Remove the user's personal context from documents meant for others. A private reflection about the user's own team does not belong in a program document.
- Do not describe a plan as proven. Plans are proposals until they have run. Say "targets are set after the first cohort".
- Keep status words honest in visuals: "Needs a nudge" and "Quiet" are fine, "Failing" is not.
- Name roles by what they do: "Communicator posts the updates and builds the demo."

## Banned words and phrases

demystify, identify (use "spot" or "find"), leverage, journey, empower, crucial, optimize, unlock, elevate, seamless, game-changer, dive in, landscape, robust, holistic, synergy.
Banned patterns: "Here's", "Here is", "Here are", "one of two", "a few things", "two things", "not ... but", "isn't ... it's", "It's not", "worth noting", em dash, parenthetical side notes like "(this is important)".

## Final scan

Run `python scripts/scan_copy.py <file.html>`. It checks the items below. Fix every hit, then check by eye what a script cannot:

- Is every section heading a question that names its thing and situation? Is the answer in the first line under it?
- Could the reader explain each section's answer in two or three sentences after reading it?
- Is every term explained where it first appears, and every "this" or "it" pinned to a named thing?
- Does any heading name a slot instead of the content?
- Does any count not match its list?
- Does any paragraph run over three sentences?
- Does any single-word sentence remain?
- Does the bold alone tell the story?
