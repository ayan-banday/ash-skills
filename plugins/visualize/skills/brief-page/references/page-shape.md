# Page shape

A brief page is read alone, once, by someone who would rather not read. It works when that reader can close the tab and say: "I know what Ash wants to do, why, what it will look like, and what he needs from me." This file covers the default order, the top screen, how each section is built, and the close.

## The default order

1. **Top screen:** the question the page answers, who it's for, read time, and what the session will decide.
2. **The short answer:** three sentences. A reader who stops here still knows the plan.
3. **One section per reader question,** in the order they'd ask it. Usually: what's wrong today, what the new thing is (in plain words), how it's organised, what changes, how it will look, how we get there.
4. **"How will it look?"** whenever something is being built or changed. It sits after the reader understands the parts, before the steps.
5. **What's still open:** the questions Ash hasn't settled, said plainly, so the reader doesn't mistake them for decisions.
6. **The close:** what the reader decides in the session, with Ash's recommendation circled.

Drop a step the content has nothing for. Never add a section without a question the reader would ask. Five to eight sections is the usual length; past ten, split the page or cut.

## The top screen

Sized to its content, with generous space above, so the short answer starts just below it on a laptop screen. A full-height opener leaves an empty band in screenshots and link previews. It holds:

- **A small line naming the page type and reader:** "Brief for Mohit · before our next session · 6-minute read".
- **The title: the page's main question,** naming the thing and the situation. "How will Ash pack his Claude skills into five plugins, and what changes for Mohit?"
- **One or two sentences** saying what the reader will know by the end.
- **"You'll decide" box:** the one to three decisions waiting in the session, one line each. This is what turns a document into a briefing.
- **One drawing** that previews the whole page (the finished thing, or the before and after in miniature). Optional, small, never decoration.

## The short answer

Three plain sentences, first thing under the top screen: **what's happening, why, and what it means for the reader.** Bold one phrase in each. No heading other than a plain label ("The short answer").

## Section anatomy: one question, one answer

Every section follows the same rhythm, so the reader learns it once:

1. **Heading:** the reader's question (rules in `writing-rules.md`).
2. **The answer, first, in one or two sentences,** with the key phrase in bold. Someone who reads only this line has the answer.
3. **The drawing** that shows the answer's shape, with one line saying what the shape means ("Each cord is one skill; its socket is the plugin it moves into").
4. **The detail:** what it means for the reader, the reason behind it, the example. Two to four short paragraphs or a small grid beside the drawing.
5. **Optional "Still open":** one line, when part of the answer isn't settled.

**The answer test.** After a section, the reader can say its answer in two or three sentences of their own. If they could only repeat the labels on the drawing, the section failed: add the reason or the example it is missing.

**The stay-on-question test.** Before keeping any paragraph, ask: does it help answer this section's question? If not, cut it or move it to the section whose question it answers.

## The "how will it look?" section

Ash calls "How does it look?" one of the best questions. Answer it whenever the page describes something being built, changed or set up.

- **Heading:** "How will [the thing] look once [the change] is done?" Name both.
- **Show the finished thing itself,** drawn as plainly as possible: the screen, the folder, the form, the message, the schedule, filled in with real or realistic content. Patterns: the real deliverable built, a before and after in the same frame, an annotated mockup (`visual-patterns.md`, "How it looks").
- **Label it "Example view"** when it is a mockup, and keep invented details plausible and listed in the hand-off note.
- **Point at two or three parts** with pen arrows or numbered callouts, each saying what the reader will notice or do there.

Describing the parts in words is never enough. If the reader couldn't sketch the result after this section, add the picture.

## The close: the decisions, with a pick circled

The page ends where the session starts.

- **Heading:** "What does Ash need [reader] to decide in [the session]?"
- **One row per decision:** the decision as a question, the two to four real options, and Ash's recommendation hand-circled with the pen circle, plus one line on why.
- **What happens after:** one line on what Ash does with the answers and by when (relative time: "the day after we talk").
- **No "Let me know your thoughts".** The reader should know exactly what to bring.

## What to leave out

- **Presenting cues:** "As I'll explain", "we'll walk through", speaker notes, a slide-like "Agenda". The page is the explanation.
- **Slot sections:** "Overview", "Background", "Next steps" as headings. Each section is a question.
- **A closing recap** that repeats the short answer. The decisions are the close.
- **Things the reader can't act on or doesn't need:** history of how the idea evolved, every alternative considered (one line on the main rejected one is enough).

## When Ash gives his own structure

Use his sections, in his order. Still phrase each heading as the reader's question, still lead each section with the answer, and still add "How will it look?" and the decisions if he left them out and the content has them. Say in the hand-off note what you added.
