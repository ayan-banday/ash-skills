Part 1

The 14-day onboarding plan

This part follows a normal student from the day they join to the day they present.

1.1 · Starting point

Students join excited and have nothing to do yet

Most students sign up because they want real work experience, and many expect an internship with a company. On Day 1 they have energy and little else.

What they bring

  • Energy to start
  • A degree and a campus
  • A reason they signed up

What they're missing

  • A team
  • A written plan
  • A first step
  • A date to work toward
1.2 · Motivation

Nobody has told students what Udyaan gives them

Each student wants something different. Today every student guesses what Udyaan offers, and many guess wrong and drift away.

In onboarding, every student writes down what they want. They answer it in the interview before Day 1, and again in a personal plan by Day 4.

That plan decides how we measure them and what they can earn.

1.3 · Who joins

Under six partners can't give 80 students real work

Out of every 100 students, about 80 want the internship model, about 15 want to build their own venture, and about 5 join the core team.

Internship, about 80 Entrepreneurship, about 15 Core team, about 5

Udyaan has under six industry partners. Most internship students have no company problem to work on, so onboarding gives them real work that doesn't depend on a partner.

This document covers the general onboarding. The core team and the entrepreneurship track run separately.

1.4 · Problems and what they cost

Without a structure, excitement leaks at six points

A student who joins today meets these problems in roughly this order. Each one has a cause and a cost.

2

Cost: they wait, and the excitement fades.

3

Why: the outcome was never defined.

Cost: no reason to stay, and nothing to measure.

4

Why: deadlines come from nobody.

Cost: work stalls even when we stay in touch.

5

Works with nobody around

Why: no team and no regular contact.

Cost: a student can go quiet and no one notices.

6

Gets no credit

Why: friends haven't heard of Udyaan, and nothing marks the effort.

Cost: no pride, and no pull to stay.

The total cost: students drop off quietly, their effort is wasted, and partners who arrive later meet students without a method.

1.10 · One student's 14 days · School of Computer Science

Meera ships a lost and found board in 14 days

Meera is a second-year Computer Science student. She expected a company internship and has no partner waiting. Scroll to watch her project build up on the right.

Before Day 1

She fills the interview and runs the prompt

She writes that she wants to learn how to talk to users and get better at presenting. The data puts her with three other Computer Science students from her campus.

Day 1

She meets her team and hears the date

The Day 14 event is fixed, so the finish line is clear on day one. Her team looks at the brief options and she posts her first update: "Team formed."

Days 2 to 3

The team writes the project doc

They pick a lost and found board for campus. They write the problem, who it helps, and what "done" means. Meera takes the Communicator role, which means she posts the updates and builds the demo.

They give every milestone a date and a name.

Day 4 · Gate 1

She drafts her personal plan

She writes down the skills she wants, user conversations and presenting, how her data structures course helps with the board, and that she'll be measured on milestones hit, updates posted, and the demo.

Days 5 to 6

The team talks to five students

The Researcher leads the five conversations. Meera writes down the exact words students use.

"I posted in three WhatsApp groups and nobody replied."

She posts an update the same day: 5 of 5 conversations done, and 4 of 5 students never found a lost item.

Day 7 · Gate 2

A mentor and her teammates give feedback

The mentor says the milestones are clear but nobody owns the demo yet. Meera takes it. In the team feedback session, a teammate asks her to post updates earlier in the day.

Days 8 to 11

The team builds and tests the board

The Builder makes a report form and a searchable sheet with a free no-code tool. The team tests it with 10 entries, fixes two bugs, and posts two updates a week.

Day 12

She rehearses the 3-minute demo

Meera times herself with her teammates watching. They tell her the middle runs long, so she cuts 40 seconds from the section on what students said.

Day 14 · Gate 3

She demos, submits her plan, and wins an award

Judges and peers watch the demo. Her team wins Most Useful Updates. Meera hands in her final plan, gives each teammate feedback, and reaches Level 1.

1.12 · The team of four

Four students per team, each with one role

A team gives each student a buddy from their own campus and a group that notices when someone goes quiet. The interview data suggests the groups, and each team then picks its project from the brief options.

L

Lead

Keeps the project doc and milestones current and runs the weekly check.

B

Builder or collector

Makes the prototype, or gathers the data.

R

Researcher

Runs the five conversations, or designs the measurements.

C

Communicator

Posts the updates and builds the demo.

Roles rotate

After the first week, students can swap roles so each one practises each skill.

A weak link shows early

  • Every role has a name next to it in the project doc.
  • Each person's updates show on the dashboard.
  • The weekly feedback session gives teammates a place to say what is going wrong.

What we give each team

  • A brief matched to their school
  • The project doc and plan templates
  • A mentor check-in on Day 7
  • A slot at the Day 14 event
1.13 · Mentor and peer feedback

Feedback comes from a mentor, teammates, and an audience

Giving feedback is a skill, so students practise it on teammates every week.

Mentor

Day 7 check-in

  • Reads the project doc and the dated milestones
  • Compares progress to the plan
  • Unblocks the team and comments on plan drafts
Teammates

Weekly team feedback session

  • Held on Day 7 and Day 14
  • Each student tells every teammate one thing to keep and one thing to change
  • Notes go on the dashboard
Audience

Day 14 demo feedback

  • Judges and other teams respond after each demo
  • Teammates rate how the project went
  • Students hear an audience's reaction for the first time

Regular feedback keeps a team honest. It also teaches students to hear a comment without taking it personally.

1.14 · Presentation skills

Every student presents for three minutes in front of people

Presenting is one of the main skills students leave with. The Day 14 event is built around it.

Events

  • Game session: teams compete in a one-hour challenge such as a quiz built from their own projects or a build-in-an-hour.

Why it works

People stay when they can see progress and when their friends can see it too. Levels give that progress a name, and events give it an audience.

2.3 · Culture

Honest and engaged is the culture we're building

The culture shows up in small habits, and onboarding teaches those habits on day one.

Honest

  • An update says what is actually done.
  • Feedback names a real problem and a fix.
  • A missed milestone gets said out loud and reset with a new date.
  • Recognition goes to people who earned it.

Engaged

  • Everyone has a role, a date, and people who notice.
  • Events are things students want to attend.
  • Students teach each other.
  • The team is present when someone needs help.

Part 3

The high-agency path

This part covers students who take more initiative. "High agency" is an internal term, and students never see it.

3.1 · Students define what they get

High-agency students set their own goals and we build around them

These students already know where they want to go, such as becoming a scientist or doing drug research. Udyaan work is one part of their growth, and the rest comes from labs, faculty, academics, and stretch goals.

How we spot them

Onboarding is the same for everyone. Initiative shows up during the 14 days: a plan that goes beyond the template, extra updates, helping teammates, and asking for harder problems.

Once we spot it, we offer a bigger plan and more responsibility, including a role the student designs.

Step 1

Spot

Initiative shows in the 14 days.

Step 2

Growth plan

Goal, skill ladder, academic map, resources.

Step 3

Access

Labs, faculty, tools, and data, each with a learning contract.

Step 4

Learn loop

Weekly log, monthly artifact, monthly review.

Step 5

Lead

Mentor teams, run events, write briefs.

3.2 · Growth plan

A growth plan starts with the incentives, then sets milestones for each

The standard personal plan has four questions. The growth plan adds five more pieces.

The standard plan, for everyone

  • Which skills am I aiming to build?
  • What am I trying to learn?
  • How will I use my degree and academic knowledge?
  • How will I be measured?

What the growth plan adds

  • Long-term goal: where the student wants to be in 3 to 5 years.
  • Skill ladder: the skills on the way, in order, with a target level each month.
  • Academic map: each course matched to the Udyaan or lab work that uses it.
  • Resources wanted: labs, a faculty guide, tools, data, and books.
  • Incentives first: what the student wants out of Udyaan, with milestones tied to each one.

The first growth plan is ours. It lists what we want to learn from Udyaan, which is product, project management, scorecards, dashboards, and distribution, plus what we want to take away and the milestones for each. We run it through the same template and review rhythm, fix what is clumsy, and then offer it to students.

3.3 · Lab access and academics

A learning contract ties every lab session to something learned

When a student gets lab access, they sign a learning contract. It's a one-page agreement between the student and a supervisor.

The learning contract has five parts

  • A question: what the student wants to find out or build.
  • A supervisor: a faculty or lab member who agrees to guide them.
  • Rules and safety: the lab's own requirements, confirmed before the first session.
  • A weekly log: what I did, what I learned, what is stuck.
  • A deliverable: a short report, a poster, or a repeat of a known result, reviewed by the supervisor.

Class and Udyaan feed each other

  • Each course gets a line in the academic map that says where the student uses it.
  • Where a course allows it, a project or lab report counts as an assignment.
  • Once a month the student answers two questions: what from class did I use, and what from Udyaan connects back to class?
  • Study time stays protected, so grades hold up.

Beyond Udyaan work

  • Own-question time: a regular block for a problem the student cares about.
  • Field mentor: someone in the student's target field, met once a month.
  • Reading and skill track: a short reading list and one skill to practise each month.
  • Outside exposure: competitions, talks, papers, and conferences.
  • Teaching: mentoring a team deepens the student's own understanding.
  • Public portfolio: one visible output each month.

Example: a Biology student

Her goal is drug research. Her team's updates are the most detailed in the cohort, and her plan names her target field. She gets lab access with a faculty supervisor, a question about a known compound, a weekly log, and a short report at the end of the month.

Her academic map ties pharmacology and biochemistry to the lab work. A mentor in the field meets her monthly, and she guides a first-year team through its Day 14 project.

Logs, artifacts, and monthly reviews show whether a student is learning

We count evidence of learning. Hours spent don't count. If the logs and artifacts stop, access pauses and we talk with the student about why.
